# MTOR: Generalizable AI-Generated Video Detection with Multimodal Semantics and Temporal Over-Regularity 

Hang Wang, Member, IEEE, Chao Shen, Fellow, IEEE, Lei Zhang, Fellow, IEEE, and Zhi-Qi Cheng, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —The rapid evolution of video generation has nar-rowed the perceptual gap between authentic and synthetic videos, making generalizable AI-generated video detection in-creasingly challenging. Existing detectors predominantly rely on visual representations, leaving caption-derived textual semantics underexplored. Meanwhile, temporal regularity in fine-grained visual representations has received limited attention. We find that caption-derived textual representations provide comple-mentary discriminative cues to global visual representations. Our analysis further reveals that AI-generated videos exhibit stronger temporal persistence and lower temporal variability, a pattern we term temporal over-regularity (TOR). Based on these findings, we propose MTOR with a multimodal branch and a TOR component. The multimodal branch integrates global visual and caption-derived textual representations, while the TOR component models temporal over-regularity at three levels: coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token correspondence, and frame-to-video stability. Extensive evaluations on five bench-marks covering 46 generator variants demonstrate state-of-the-art overall performance against 16 representative baselines, while robustness experiments confirm strong resilience to twelve real-world video perturbations. Code and models will be released. 0

Index Terms —AI-generated video detection, multimodal se-mantics, temporal over-regularity, cross-generator generalization.

The rapid evolution of generative artificial intelligence has spurred unprecedented breakthroughs in video synthesis. Driven by scaling laws and advanced diffusion-based archi-tectures, state-of-the-art video generation frameworks such as Sora [1], Runway Gen-4 [2] and Kling [3] are capable of producing high-fidelity videos with photorealistic visual quality, fluent motion, and coherent spatiotemporal scenes. These advances have made AI-generated videos increasingly difficult to visually distinguish from authentic videos. Consequently, developing a generalizable detection paradigm to identify AI-generated videos has become increasingly important for ad-vancing multimedia forensics and safeguarding societal trust. To mitigate these threats, recent studies have explored diverse temporal and spatiotemporal modeling strategies for AI-generated video detection. To address these limitations, we investigate AI-generated video detection from two complementary perspectives: video-level multimodal semantics and fine-grained temporal regu-larity. First, we examine the contribution of caption-derived textual representations when combined with global visual rep-resentations. Second, we analyze fine-grained visual dynamics at three levels: coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token correspondence, and frame-to-video stability. Across these levels, AI-generated videos con-sistently exhibit stronger temporal persistence and reduced temporal variability than real-world videos. To model this pattern, we develop a TOR component with six temporal descriptors spanning coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token corre-spondence, and frame-to-video stability. 3) Extensive experiments on five benchmarks covering 46 video generator variants demonstrate strong cross-generator performance against 16 representative base-lines. R ELATED WORK 

A. Deepfake Video Detection 

Deepfake video detection has gradually shifted from frame-level artifact analysis toward exploiting temporal and spa-tiotemporal inconsistencies across video frames. Early video-based methods therefore focused on exposing inter-frame inconsistencies. STIL [18] integrates spatial inconsistencies with adjacent-frame temporal differences, while FTCN [19] suppresses spatial modeling to emphasize temporal features and captures long-range temporal coherence with a Tempo-ral Transformer. Subsequent studies further model temporal dependencies over multiple ranges. MRE-Net [20] employs multi-rate sampling to capture short- and long-term spatiotem-poral inconsistencies, whereas ISTVT [21] decomposes self-attention into spatial and temporal dimensions and emphasizes inter-frame temporal inconsistencies. TALL [22] rearranges consecutive frames into a compact thumbnail for efficient spatiotemporal modeling, while MINTIME [23] introduces identity-aware modeling for videos containing multiple iden-tities and varying face sizes. As deepfake generation techniques become increasingly realistic, improving generalization to unseen manipulations has received growing attention. AltFreezing [24] alternately freezes spatial- and temporal-related weights to prevent ex-cessive reliance on either artifact type, while DIP [25] mod-els directional spatiotemporal inconsistencies and introduces generalization-oriented training strategies. Beyond conven-tional RGB-level temporal cues, StyleFlow [26] analyzes temporal variations of facial style latent vectors, and recent work [27] extends temporal analysis to the frequency domain. Yan et al. [28] identify Facial Feature Drift as a transferable temporal forgery artifact and construct hard negative samples through video-level blending, whereas FakeSTormer [29] in-troduces auxiliary spatial and temporal vulnerability branches to improve generalization and interpretability. Collectively, these studies seek more transferable temporal representations and learning strategies for generalized deepfake detection. Multimodal cues have also been investigated for deepfake video detection. Feng et al. [30] formulate video forensics as self-supervised anomaly detection based on audio-visual syn-chronization, while AVFF [9] learns audio-visual correspon-dences through self-supervised contrastive and reconstruction objectives before supervised fine-tuning. These studies demon-strate that inconsistencies in audio-visual synchronization and correspondence can help distinguish manipulated from authen-tic videos. Nevertheless, most deepfake video detectors are designed for facial manipulation and primarily exploit face-specific spatial, temporal, or audio-visual cues. Their problem setting therefore differs from open-domain AI-generated video detection, where entire videos may be synthesized by diverse generation models and span a wide range of scenes, objects, actions, and motion patterns. 

B. AI-Generated Video Detection 

Unlike deepfake detection, which primarily targets facial manipulation, AI-generated video detection addresses fully synthesized videos beyond the facial domain, making gener-alization to unseen video generators particularly challenging. Early studies approached this problem by modeling spatial and temporal artifacts in generated videos. AIGVDet [31] employs separate RGB and optical-flow branches to capture spatial abnormalities and temporal inconsistencies, respectively, and combines their frame-level predictions through decision-level fusion for video-level detection. DeMamba [32] further models local spatiotemporal inconsistencies across different regions using a structured state-space model. However, spatial artifacts are often generator-specific. Motivated by this observation, DeCoF [13] reduces the influence of spatial artifacts during feature learning and focuses on inter-frame consistency to improve generalization to unseen generators. Together, these studies highlight spatial and temporal artifacts as important forensic cues for AI-generated video detection. More recent studies have examined video dynamics from increasingly diverse perspectives. ReStraV [14] analyzes frame trajectories in a pretrained visual representation space and characterizes their geometry through temporal curvature and stepwise distance. D3 [15] derives second-order temporal features from first-order inter-frame differences and performs training-free detection using statistics of second-order central differences. III. E MPIRICAL OBSERVATIONS AND MOTIVATION 

This section presents the empirical observations underlying the design of MTOR. We first examine video-level visual and caption-derived textual representations for AI-generated video detection and show that combining the two improves detection over either representation alone. We then analyze temporal dy-namics reflected in fine-grained visual representations and ob-serve that AI-generated videos exhibit stronger temporal per-sistence and reduced temporal variability, a tendency we term 

temporal over-regularity (TOR). Finally, experiments across five benchmarks show that TOR complements the video-level multimodal representation, motivating the joint modeling of multimodal semantics and temporal over-regularity in MTOR. 

A. Video-Level Multimodal Representation 

To examine the effectiveness of different video-level rep-resentations for AI-generated video detection, we evaluate three variants: visual-only, text-only, and a multimodal variant 

G that combines the two representations. Given an input video V, a pretrained video encoder extracts its global visual representation, which is used by the visual-only variant for authenticity classification. As reported in Table I, this variant achieves 97.42% AP and 97.04% AUC on GenVideo. We next examine the caption-derived textual representation. A video captioning model generates a textual description from the same input video, which is subsequently encoded into a video-level textual representation. Since the caption is generated directly from V, no external textual input is required. As shown in Table I, the text-only variant achieves 93 .18% AP and 92 .81% AUC. Although both representations are derived from the same video, they encode the video from different perspectives. The visual representation encodes global spatiotemporal in-formation, whereas the caption-derived textual representation summarizes high-level semantics such as objects, actions, attributes, and their relations. Combining the two increases AP from 97 .42% to 98 .81% , AUC from 97 .04% to 98 .66% ,and ACC from 90 .30% to 93 .80% . These gains indicate that caption-derived textual representations provide complementary discriminative cues to global visual representations. Accord-ingly, MTOR jointly models the two representations in its video-level multimodal branch G, while fine-grained temporal dynamics are investigated next. B. Temporal Over-Regularity 

To investigate temporal regularity in fine-grained visual representations, we analyze visual tokens at three levels, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a): coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token correspondence, and frame-to-video stability. Each level is characterized by statistics reflecting temporal per-sistence and variability. Fig. 1(b)–(d) visualize the distributions of real and AI-generated videos under these analyses. Across all three levels, AI-generated videos exhibit stronger temporal persistence and reduced temporal variability than real videos. 

Coarse Inter-frame Continuity: We average the visual to-kens within each frame to obtain a frame centroid and compute the cosine similarity between consecutive frame centroids. Fig. 1(b) plots the temporal mean of adjacent-centroid similar-ity against its standard deviation. AI-generated videos exhibit higher mean similarity and lower temporal variation, indicating stronger inter-frame continuity. 

Fine-grained Token Correspondence: We compare visual tokens between consecutive frames to characterize fine-grained temporal correspondence. For each pair of adjacent frames, each token is matched to its most similar counterpart in the other frame, and the resulting similarities are aggregated into an adjacent-token correspondence score. Fig. 1(c) shows that AI-generated videos exhibit higher mean correspondence and lower temporal variation, indicating stronger and more stable token correspondence across consecutive frames. 

Frame-to-video Stability: To characterize temporal regular-ity over a broader context, we compare the visual tokens of each frame with those from the remaining frames in the video, yielding a frame-to-video similarity. Fig. 1(d) characterizes the temporal variation of these similarities. AI-generated videos exhibit lower variation and smaller temporal changes, indicat-ing more stable frame-to-video relations over time. Collectively, these three analyses reveal a consistent tempo-ral tendency in AI-generated videos, characterized by stronger temporal persistence and reduced temporal variability. We term this tendency temporal over-regularity (TOR), which motivates the TOR component for AI-generated video detection.

The multimodal representation captures global visual and caption-derived semantics, whereas TOR character-izes temporal over-regularity reflected in fine-grained visual dynamics. We therefore examine whether the two provide complementary cues for AI-generated video detection. Table II reports the mean performance across five bench-marks: GenVideo, EvalCrafter, VideoPhy, VidProM, and Gen-VidBench. The multimodal branch G achieves 97.26% AP, 97.17% AUC, and 89.51% ACC, while the TOR-only variant achieves 93.22% AP, 94.59% AUC, and 84.19% ACC. By combining these two predictions, MTOR improves perfor-mance to 97.76% AP, 97.92% AUC, and 90.24% ACC. These gains indicate that temporal over-regularity provides discrim-inative cues beyond the global multimodal representation. These results demonstrate the complementarity between video-level multimodal semantics and temporal over-regularity. Accordingly, MTOR combines the predictions from the multimodal branch and the TOR component through score-level fusion, as described in Sec. IV. IV. M ETHODOLOGY 

Motivated by the empirical observations in Sec. III, MTOR jointly models video-level multimodal semantics and temporal over-regularity for AI-generated video detection. The mul-timodal branch integrates global visual and caption-derived textual representations, whereas the TOR component models temporal over-regularity through coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token correspondence, and frame-to-video stabil-ity. The two components produce complementary detection scores, which are calibrated using source-training statistics and subsequently fused to obtain the final MTOR score.

A. Datasets 

We evaluate MTOR on five benchmarks covering di-verse video generators and data distributions, including Gen-Video [32], EvalCrafter [33], VideoPhy [34], VidProM [35], and GenVidBench [36]. 

Training Set. We use the official training split of GenVideo as the source dataset and further divide it into source-training and source-validation subsets. Real videos are sampled from the Youku-mPLUG dataset [37], and synthetic videos are generated by Pika [38]. 

Validation Set. We randomly reserve 20% of the source data as a stratified validation set for learning-rate scheduling, early stopping, and hyperparameter selection. 

Testing Set. The testing set collects synthetic videos from 46 generator variants across five benchmarks, with real videos uniformly sampled from MSR-VTT [39] following Gen-Video’s standard setting.

D. Evaluation Metrics 

Following prior state-of-the-art methods, such as Re-StraV [14], NSG-VD [16], DeMamba [32], and DeCoF [13], we adopt three widely used metrics to evaluate our approach: Average Precision (AP), Area Under the Receiver Operat-ing Characteristic curve (AUC), and Accuracy (ACC). AP evaluates the precision-recall trade-off via the area under the precision-recall curve, reflecting the model’s ability to precisely rank synthetic videos. AUC quantifies the overall detection capability across all possible thresholds, representing the probability that a randomly chosen AI-generated video is scored higher than a real one. ACC measures the proportion of correctly classified real and AI-generated videos. Throughout Tables III–XIX, bold and underlined values denote the best and second-best results within each column, respectively.

In this paper, we proposed MTOR for generalizable AI-generated video detection by jointly considering video-level multimodal semantics and temporal over-regularity. The mul-timodal branch represented each video from both visual and caption-derived semantic perspectives, while retaining the video itself as the sole input. Our temporal analysis further revealed that AI-generated videos tended to exhibit stronger temporal persistence and reduced temporal variability, which we termed temporal over-regularity. To quantify this temporal pattern, we developed the TOR component with six descrip-tors characterizing coarse inter-frame continuity, fine-grained token correspondence, and frame-to-video stability. The pre-dictions from the two branches were combined through source-calibrated score fusion using only source-domain statistics. Across five large-scale benchmarks and 46 generator variants, comparisons with 16 representative baselines demonstrated JOURNAL OF L ATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 16 

the state-of-the-art overall performance of MTOR. Additional ablation studies confirmed the complementary contributions of the multimodal branch and TOR component, while robustness experiments showed consistently strong performance across twelve diverse video perturbations. Overall, these results estab-lished MTOR as an effective and robust framework for cross-generator AI-generated video detection. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS
