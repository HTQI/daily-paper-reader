Abstract—Deepfake detection in videos remains challenging, as
manipulated content may appear visually consistent at the frame
level while exhibiting subtle temporal inconsistencies. This paper
introduces an interpretable deepfake detection framework that
models spatially and temporally coherent facial features in video
sequences. Unlike end-to-end deep models relying on implicit
representations, the proposed approach explicitly encodes phys-
ically grounded forensic cues, enabling transparent analysis and
improved multi-dataset generalization. The pipeline transforms
videos into identity-consistent facial trajectories, segments them
into fixed-length temporal windows, and represents each frame
using 68 structured descriptors spanning four complementary do-
mains: photometric, textural, geometric, and compression-based
features. These descriptors provide a compact multi-domain
representation of manipulation artifacts and are processed by a
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to capture temporal
dependencies and subtle irregularities. Evaluation on four bench-
mark datasets, FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF v2, a curated subset of
the DeepFake Detection Challenge (DFDC), and DeeperForensics,
yields strong and consistent F1-scores of 98.0%, 91.0%, 97.6%,
and 96.2%, respectively. The approach also demonstrated a good
cross-dataset generalization, providing a robust and interpretable
solution for video deepfake detection.
Index Terms—Deepfake detection in videos, forensic features,
Temporal modelling, LSTM, Explainable AI.
I. INTRODUCTION
The rapid evolution of generative models, particularly
GANs and diffusion models, has significantly increased the
realism and accessibility of deepfake videos, posing major
challenges for multimedia forensics [1]. Modern pipelines
generate highly realistic faces even under real-world con-
ditions such as compression, resizing, and motion, making
frame-level detection increasingly unreliable [2]. The deep-
fake landscape now spans diverse forgery types and post-
processing conditions, exposing the limitations of methods
tailored to narrow benchmarks. Consequently, recent research
has shifted toward modeling temporal consistency, improving
cross-domain generalization, and enhancing explainability for
trustworthy forensic analysis [3], [4].
Recent studies show that while spatial artifacts can be
minimized at the frame level, deepfake generation often
fails to preserve temporal consistency in videos, leading to
subtle irregularities in motion, appearance transitions, and
identity coherence across frames [5], [6]. These inconsisten-
cies, unstable facial dynamics, illumination shifts, geometric
distortions, or evolving compression traces, make temporal
modeling essential for robust detection [7]. However, many
existing approaches rely on end-to-end deep representations
that lack interpretability and may overfit to dataset-specific
artifacts, limiting forensic reliability and cross-dataset gener-
alization [8], [9]. Furthermore, temporal models commonly
process independently sampled frames without preserving
identity-consistent facial trajectories, introducing background
variations and unrelated visual content into the learned rep-
resentation [6], [10], [11]. These limitations highlight a key
weakness: manipulated frames may appear realistic in isola-
tion, while their inconsistencies become apparent only when
forensic cues are analyzed coherently over time.
Motivated by these limitations, we propose a contextual
deepfake detection framework that models spatiotemporal con-
sistency through structured and interpretable representations.
Instead of relying on fully end-to-end processing, the pipeline
is decomposed into principled stages: faces are first detected
and tracked into identity-consistent trajectories, segmented
into fixed-length temporal windows, and encoded at each
frame using 68 semantically grounded features spanning pho-
tometric, textural, geometric, and compression characteristics.
These feature categories are deliberately selected to capture
complementary traces of face manipulation. They also encom-
pass the principal visual inconsistencies commonly associated
with face synthesis, blending, and post-processing [6], [12].
Photometric descriptors characterize inconsistencies in color,
illumination, and local intensity statistics; textural features
capture abnormal skin patterns and fine-scale artifacts result-
ing from generative reconstruction [13]; geometric descrip-
tors encode distortions or instabilities in facial structure and
landmark relationships [14]; and compression features reveal
discrepancies caused by repeated encoding, resampling, and
manipulation-specific post-processing.
Temporal dependencies among frame-level representations
are then modeled using an LSTM to identify persistent incon-
sistencies, abrupt variations, and unnatural temporal evolution
[15]. The originality of the proposed approach lies in com-

bining explicit forensic descriptors with temporal sequence
learning over identity-consistent trajectories. This enables the
model to capture coherent temporal patterns overlooked by
frame-based methods while reducing overfitting to dataset-
specific visual cues. Moreover, the use of physically mean-
ingful features improves interpretability by linking detection
decisions to observable forensic inconsistencies. Our contribu-
tions are summarized as follows:
• A complete pipeline that transforms raw videos into ho-
mogeneous, identity-preserving face sequences, ensuring
temporal coherence and avoiding frame-level ambiguities.
The method introduces a compact set of 68 interpretable
forensic features across photometric, textural, geometric,
and compression cues, providing a structured and phys-

with improved robustness and interpretability.
• A unified spatiotemporal framework that encodes each
identity-consistent track as a fixed T × 68 matrix, stan-

inconsistencies beyond frame-level analysis. Extensive
evaluation on FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF v2, DFDC, and
DeeperForensics demonstrates strong and consistent per-
formance across diverse conditions. Cross-dataset evalu-
ation on ForgeryNet and WildDeepfake further demon-
strates the framework ability to generalize to real-world
deepfake distributions.

II. RECENT LITERATURE AND POSITIONING
Recent work has shifted deepfake video detection toward
more realistic and comprehensive evaluation settings. Recent
benchmarking efforts unify tasks, datasets, and metrics, high-
lighting the need for robust evaluation under rapidly evolving
generative models [1]. Similarly, Celeb-DF++ emphasizes
cross-dataset testing across diverse forgery types, compres-
sion levels, and visual qualities [16]. These studies demon-
strate that performance on a single benchmark is insufficient,
as detection difficulty varies considerably across generation
pipelines and post-processing conditions. In parallel, several
approaches explicitly address cross-dataset generalization [17],
[18]. Overall, these works establish generalization and multi-
benchmark evaluation as central requirements for reliable
deepfake detection.
A second major trend is the shift from static spatial analysis
toward temporal and contextual modeling. Recent approaches
exploit global and local temporal consistency, spatiotemporal
patterns, and frequency-based cues to capture artifacts that
remain invisible at the frame level [8], [9], [19]. These studies
reinforce the view that videos should be treated as dynamic
signals rather than collections of independent frames. Beyond
temporal cues, contextual modeling has gained importance,
with methods incorporating multi-face and scene-level rela-
tionships or combining multi-view and temporal inconsisten-
cies for detection and localization [20], [21]. These works
highlight the importance of relational and contextual informa-
tion in realistic scenarios. Finally, interpretability is emerging
as a key requirement, with recent work combining temporal
modeling with more transparent decision mechanisms [22].
In this context, the present work positions itself at the
intersection of temporal modeling and explainability, propos-
ing a sequence-based framework that operates on explicit,
semantically meaningful features. Rather than replacing end-
to-end architectures, it offers a complementary and more
interpretable paradigm for deepfake video forensics.

E. LSTM-Based Deepfake Prediction
Given the structured temporal representation X ∈R20×68,
the prediction task consists in modeling the temporal evolution
of explicit forensic variables to identify inconsistencies indica-
tive of deepfake generation. Unlike frame-based approaches,
the objective here is not only to detect anomalous values, but
to capture their dynamics over time, such as instability, drift,
or incoherent co-evolution across feature families.
To this end, we employ a Long Short-Term Memory
(LSTM) network, which is well-suited for sequential data with
temporal dependencies. The LSTM processes the sequence
frame by frame, allowing the model to learn both short-
term variations and longer-range temporal patterns within
the tracked face trajectory. This is particularly relevant in
our setting, where deepfake artifacts often manifest as subtle
temporal inconsistencies rather than strong spatial distortions.

The first LSTM layer (128 units) returns full sequences,
allowing the second LSTM layer (64 units) to capture higher-

the final hidden state, which is then passed to the fully

include dropout regularization (rate = 0.2) to improve gener-

This design captures both short-term fluctuations and long-
range dependencies within the sequence, while preserving
interactions across feature families. By modeling the temporal
evolution of explicit variables rather than latent embeddings,
the network focuses on interpretable forensic patterns such as
instability, drift, and cross-feature inconsistencies. The model


is trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10−3, a batch size of 32, and a maximum of 35 epochs, with
early stopping (patience of 5) based on validation loss. The
final classification uses a sigmoid activation optimized with
binary cross-entropy loss. The full pipeline runs in real time,
processing each video frame within the latency budget required
for live deployment.
V. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
A. Dataset-Wise Corpus Construction and Standardization
The protocol uses four widely adopted deepfake video
datasets: FaceForensics++ [3], Celeb-DF v2 [30], a subset
of the DeepFake Detection Challenge dataset (DFDC) [31],
and DeeperForensics [32]. Unlike a merged-corpus protocol,
the proposed methodology is applied independently to each
dataset. Each dataset therefore undergoes the same preprocess-
ing, tracking, sequence construction, feature extraction, and
LSTM-based classification pipeline, but the reported results
are dataset-specific.
Having multiple datasets reflects different manipulation
pipelines, acquisition conditions, compression levels, and vi-
sual realism. It also avoids hiding dataset-specific difficulty
behind a single aggregate score. The method is therefore eval-
uated not only by its strongest result, but also by its behavior
across heterogeneous data sources. The fixed parameters of
the protocol are:
• Harmonized frame rate: 25 fps after resampling;
• Final face crop size: 224 × 224 pixels;
• Sequence length: 20 consecutive frames. One sequence
corresponds to one tracked face only
• Sequence stride: 10 frames;
For each dataset, preprocessing converts raw videos into
standardized face trajectories by discarding low-quality detec-
tions, resizing valid face crops to 224 × 224, and generating
fixed-length temporal windows. Each retained sequence is
encoded as a 20 × 68 matrix, and the train/validation/test split
is performed at the video level to prevent temporal leakage.
This pipeline transforms heterogeneous inputs, varying in
frame rate, resolution, number of faces, and quality, into
consistent temporal samples of a single tracked identity. Such
standardization ensures that the temporal model receives inputs
with fixed sequence length, uniform spatial resolution, con-
sistent feature dimensionality, preserved temporal order, and
identity continuity, thereby avoiding unstable or semantically
inconsistent representations.
To evaluate the proposed methods, we report standard
metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score,
capturing both overall performance and class-wise behavior.
Accuracy reflects global correctness, while precision and recall
measure the reliability of positive predictions and the ability
to detect all relevant instances, particularly important in im-
balanced settings. The F1-score balances this trade-off. We
also report the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), which
assesses discriminative ability across all decision thresholds
and provides insight into robustness under varying conditions.
B. Dataset-wise Main Results
Table II reports the performance obtained when the same
proposed pipeline is applied independently to each dataset.
Unlike the global performance reported in the ablation study
(97.8% accuracy), which is computed over the complete test
set, the results presented here are evaluated separately on each
dataset and therefore reflect dataset-specific behavior rather
than a global aggregate.
The highest performance is observed on FaceForensics++,
where the method reaches 98.0% accuracy and 98.0% F1-
score, indicating that the proposed explicit feature representa-
tion effectively captures compression inconsistencies, photo-
metric imbalances, and fine-grained textural artifacts present
in this benchmark. Similarly, the DFDC subset achieves strong

C. Comparison with Recent Literature
Table IV shows a comparison of our approach with regard
to recent state-of-the art methods. Since not all works report
the same evaluation metrics, the comparison is limited to
AUC values explicitly provided in the original papers. The
comparative methods are: 1) Amin et al. [19] which focus on
temporal consistency patterns across frames, 2) Yan et al. [8]
uses spatiotemporal adaptation mechanisms to improve cross-
dataset generalization, 3)Kim et al. [9] exploits temporal-
frequency inconsistencies at the pixel level, capturing subtle
artifacts that are not observable in the spatial domain alone.
Guo et al. [17] extended this direction by explicitly modeling
temporal forgery cues, reporting both frame-wise and video-
wise performance, 5) Hu et al. [20] which incorporate contex-
tual information in multi-face scenarios (e.g. gaze alignment,
inter-person interactions, and motion coherence).
Despite differences in evaluation settings, several trends
emerge. Temporal coherence and spatiotemporal adaptation
methods [8], [19] perform strongly on controlled datasets but
degrade on challenging benchmarks such as DFDC, indicating
limited robustness to domain shifts. Frequency- and temporal-
cue approaches [9], [17] achieve competitive results but vary
across datasets, while context-aware methods [20] improve
robustness in multi-face scenarios. In contrast, the proposed
method achieves the highest average AUC (98.0%) and a
strong pooled AUC (99.2%), with consistently high precision,
recall, and F1-score. These results suggest that combining
explicit forensic features with temporal modeling provides
a complementary representation of deepfake artifacts and
improves consistency across diverse datasets.
D. Cross-Dataset Generalization
To further assess robustness and generalization, we evaluate
the proposed framework on two unseen datasets: ForgeryNet
[33] as a validation set and WildDeepfake [34] as a held-
out test set. The same pipeline and model weights learned
from the original four training datasets are applied without
retraining or fine-tuning, providing a strictly zero-shot cross-
dataset evaluation.
ForgeryNet contains diverse manipulation types under mod-
erate and relatively controlled compression conditions, making
it suitable for evaluating generalization to unseen forgery
pipelines. In contrast, WildDeepfake comprises real-world
deepfake videos collected from the Internet, with substan-
tial variability in acquisition conditions, compression levels,
video quality, and generation methods. As reported in Ta-
ble V, the model achieves 93.8% accuracy and F1-score
on ForgeryNet, and 89.4% accuracy and 89.5% F1-score on
WildDeepfake, respectively. These results demonstrate strong
cross-dataset generalization without retraining or adaptation,
indicating that the learned temporal patterns are not limited
to the distributions of the training datasets. The lower per-
formance on WildDeepfake reflects the greater difficulty of

VI. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
The proposed framework relies on explicitly designed
forensic features (photometric consistency, texture statistics,
geometric structure, and compression artifacts), which are
interpretable, computationally lightweight, and physically intu-
itive, making their discriminative behavior directly explainable
without requiring any post-hoc explanation. Integrated within
a temporally coherent tracking pipeline, these features provide
a transparent and efficient alternative to black-box deep rep-
resentations. Ensuring each sequence corresponds to a single
identity allows feature variations to reflect meaningful tempo-
ral patterns, while LSTM-based aggregation accumulates weak
frame-level cues into a robust, interpretable representation.
Strong results on several datasets confirm that representing
each sequence as a matrix of explicit forensic features enables
the model to capture temporal inconsistencies while preserving
interpretability. The approach demonstrates that physically
meaningful features, consistently tracked over time, provide an
effective basis for deepfake detection. Cross-dataset general-
ization experiments on ForgeryNet and WildDeepfake further
confirm that the explicit forensic representation transfers to
unseen data without retraining, yielding competitive results
under challenging in-the-wild conditions.
Looking ahead, several directions merit exploration. First,
extending the framework to analyze faces across arbitrary ori-
entations, scales, and pose variations could improve robustness
beyond predominantly frontal views, while modeling facial
expression dynamics may reveal unnatural affective transitions


or temporal inconsistencies. Second, reformulating the facial
landmark graph as an explicit graph neural network [35] would
allow relational reasoning over facial topology, enabling the
model to capture structural anomalies and asymmetries that
sequential models cannot directly exploit. Third, incorporating
multimodal signals by pairing facial features with speech [36],
phoneme-visual alignment, or physiological cues could expose
cross-modal inconsistencies that unimodal analysis misses.
Fourth, replacing the LSTM with a Transformer-based se-
quence model would enable direct long-range attention over
facial trajectories, capturing subtle non-local temporal anoma-
lies beyond the reach of recurrent architectures. Finally, hybrid
architectures combining the interpretable handcrafted features
introduced here with learned deep representations could im-
prove robustness to unseen manipulations while preserving the
transparency and physical grounding required for forensically
credible analysis.
