# ABSTRACT 

Multimodal rumor detectors increasingly rely on retrieved evidence, yet relevant evidence is not necessarily sufficient for verification. Missing provenance, dupli-cated reports, and unresolved contradictions can produce confident predictions without adequate support. We introduce SAFE-MR, a framework that separates claim veracity from evidence sufficiency. The method decomposes image–text posts into verifiable claims, constructs a relation-aware claim–evidence graph, and aggregates evidence using provenance and contextual compatibility. Separate veracity and sufficiency heads support selective prediction, while evidence inter-ventions encourage stability under irrelevant additions and sensitivity to evidence removal. On NewsCLIPpings, VERITE, and XFacta, SAFE-MR achieves macro-F1 scores of 91.2%, 75.8%, and 85.2%, respectively. Against the matched backbone with evidence, its macro-F1 gains are 2.2, 4.9, and 4.8 percentage points. On the diagnostic selection set, SAFE-MR reduces AURC from 0.105 for maximum-probability rejection to 0.075 and lowers error at 80% coverage from 13.8% to 8.5%. Evidence-perturbation and ablation results support the role of sufficiency learning and intervention training in improving selective verification. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Authentic images can be repurposed to support false descriptions of when, where, or why an event occurred. Such out-of-context misinformation is difficult to detect from visual appearance alone: a flood photograph may be genuine even when its location and date are false. A detector must determine whether the image supports the specific contextual claim, rather than merely whether the image and text concern similar topics. Throughout this paper, multimodal rumor detection refers to image–text veracity assessment; it does not imply that every unverified rumor is false. External retrieval offers a way to recover missing context. Evidence can reveal an image’s earlier publication or establish the identity of a depicted event. However, retrieved pages may repeat the claim under investigation, omit decisive details, or disagree about the event. The number of matching pages is especially misleading when several pages copy a single source. Consequently, evidence relevance, source independence, and evidence sufficiency must be distinguished. Recent methods provide strong foundations for evidence-based detection. SNIFFER investigates explainable out-of-context detection with multimodal language models (Qi et al., 2024); E2LVLM reorganizes retrieved evidence for model-based verification (Wu et al., 2025); TRUST-VL studies general misinformation detection across distortion types (Yan et al., 2025a). Meanwhile, simple multimodal similarity features remain competitive on established benchmarks (Papadopoulos et al., 2025). These observations motivate evaluation beyond conventional accuracy: a system can exploit retrieval similarity without establishing whether the decisive factual proposition is supported. Consider the constructed mismatch in Figure 1, which uses a real aerial flood image. The deliberately incorrect caption attributes it to City A in 2025. A topical report alone does not establish the image’s location or date. The source record instead identifies the mosaic as imagery of Mabi, Kurashiki, Japan, photographed by the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan on 9 July 2018 and subsequently assembled into a mosaic. This provenance refutes the proposed 2025 attribution. The claim’s underlying truth has not changed; the evidence available to the verifier has changed. We propose SAFE-MR to operationalize this distinction. It learns separate representations for veracity and evidence sufficiency, using atomic claims as the interface between image–text understanding and evidence reasoning. An intervention objective teaches the model which changes should preserve its output and which should reduce its willingness to decide. This is a controlled robustness objective, not a claim of identifying real-world causal effects. Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate evidence sufficiency as an evidence-conditioned target distinct from veracity and predictive confidence. Second, we develop relation-aware claim– evidence reasoning with provenance-sensitive aggregation and intervention-specific learning objec-tives. Third, we evaluate classification, evidence perturbations, selective prediction, and component ablations, showing how sufficiency modeling complements conventional confidence-based rejection. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 MULTIMODAL CONSISTENCY AND EXTERNAL VERIFICATION 

NewsCLIPpings supports the study of contextually mismatched image–text pairs (Luo et al., 2021). External-resource verification extends this setting by retrieving textual and visual evidence (Abdelnabi et al., 2022). However, similarity between a query and evidence is only a proxy for factual support. MUSE and AITR provide strong tests of whether architectural complexity contributes beyond this proxy (Papadopoulos et al., 2025). VERITE addresses unimodal biases through modality-balanced examples (Papadopoulos et al., 2023). We therefore evaluate both standard synthetic data and real-world transfer, with similarity-based and evidence-aware comparators. 2.2 VISION –L ANGUAGE MODELS FOR MISINFORMATION DETECTION 

SNIFFER (Qi et al., 2024), E2LVLM (Wu et al., 2025), and TRUST-VL (Yan et al., 2025a) motivate comparison with specialized vision–language models. Their explanations and evidence processing 2Preprint do not, by themselves, establish that confidence tracks the adequacy of the evidence supplied. XFacta additionally motivates separating retrieval quality from reasoning quality on recent real-world content (Xiao et al., 2025). SAFE-MR focuses on evidence-conditioned sufficiency supervision and intervention-sensitive selection, complementing advances in backbone modeling and evidence retrieval. 2.3 EVIDENCE QUALITY AND SELECTIVE PREDICTION 

Evidence pollution has already been investigated for out-of-context detection (Yan et al., 2025b), and FRAUD-Net considers variable or missing evidence (Patel et al., 2025). These are necessary comparators for robustness claims. Selective prediction permits a classifier to abstain (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2019), while temperature scaling offers a straightforward confidence calibration baseline (Guo et al., 2017). We compare SAFE-MR against these mechanisms applied to the same backbone. Improvements over a forced classifier alone would not establish that explicit sufficiency learning is useful.

A separate target a∗(x, E ) ∈ { 0, 1} indicates whether the supplied, admissible evidence permits a justified decision. The action space is {0, 1, ⊥} , where ⊥ denotes abstention and is not a third truth category. Annotators judge sufficiency from the supplied evidence packet, not from unrestricted web access or remembered facts. Support requires coverage of the material claims and the image–event relation. Refutation may require only one decisive contradiction to a material claim. A source count or average coverage score is therefore insufficient as a complete decision rule. Unresolved conflict is recorded separately; disagreement from a demonstrably derivative or unreliable source need not force abstention. 3.2 CLAIM EXTRACTION AND EVIDENCE PREPARATION 

A vision–language backbone extracts a set C = {ci}mi=1 of atomic propositions grounded in the post. Each proposition records a predicate, relevant entities, temporal and spatial qualifiers, and whether it is asserted by the text or inferred from visual content. Non-visible dates and locations are not treated as directly observed image facts. OCR is retained as a noisy observation with provenance. Each proposition also receives a materiality flag indicating whether contradicting it changes the post’s contextual interpretation. Textual queries combine material entities and event qualifiers, whereas image queries seek the image’s publication context. Evidence records comprise textual content, optional images, and available provenance metadata. Near-identical or syndicated reports are treated as dependent rather than independent corroboration. Missing dates remain explicitly missing, and temporal compatibility concerns the event described in the claim rather than the recency of a retrieved page. Training interventions are constructed on training evidence; analogous perturbations are evaluated separately on held-out examples. Irrelevant addition preserves label and sufficiency when annotation confirms that no material information is introduced. We penalize prediction drift with Jensen–Shannon divergence and squared sufficiency differences. Key deletion lowers the sufficiency target only when all redundant decisive evidence is removed and the remaining packet becomes insufficient. Its ranking loss is max(0 , δ − s(E) + s(E−)) , where δ = 0 .2 is the ranking margin. Conflicting addition receives packet-specific sufficiency labels: a weak repost opposing an authenti-cated archive may leave sufficiency unchanged, while two unresolved credible accounts can lower it. Pollution adds one group containing misleading evidence to a locally controlled packet, with the document’s status recorded. These interventions do not require the veracity prediction to flip toward false evidence. The truth label remains fixed, while acceptance should decrease when justification is lost. # 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 DATASETS AND TASKS 

We evaluate image–text verification on NewsCLIPpings (Luo et al., 2021), VERITE-full (Papadopou-los et al., 2023), and XFacta (Xiao et al., 2025). NewsCLIPpings provides synthetic out-of-context pairs. This study uses 100,000 training, 5,000 validation, and 5,000 test examples, with no sam-ple exclusions. Each split is balanced between authentic matches and out-of-context mismatches (50% each). These counts describe the split used in this study, rather than a universal official split. VERITE-full uses all 1,000 examples with binary labels: 338 truthful and 662 misleading examples, with both misleading categories mapped to false. The complete 1,000-example evaluation set is used throughout, without subset selection. VERITE posts do not enter model training, hyperparameter search, or temperature calibration. However, 1,200 evidence packets derived from all 1,000 posts were used before protocol freezing to develop annotation rules and clarify sufficiency judgments. Thus, VERITE is held out from model fitting but was not untouched during annotation-protocol development. XFacta (arXiv:2508.09999v1) contains 2,400 examples, comprising an official development set of 240 examples (120 true and 120 false) and an official test set of 2,160 examples (1,080 true and 1,080 false). The model-development annotation set comprises 3,000 evidence packets constructed from the 240 official XFacta development posts and 860 additionally collected posts. Its 2,400 training, 300 tuning, and 300 calibration packets are partitioned by original post, so that all variants of a post remain in one partition. Separate resources comprise 1,200 VERITE-derived packets for annotation-rule development and 100 pilot packets for annotator training, totaling 4,300 packets outside the diagnostic suite. Pilot packets enter no formal split; VERITE-derived development packets enter no model-training, tuning, or calibration split. GPT-5.6-Sol produces initial sufficiency labels, followed by individual human review. Two annotators perform first reviews, with each packet initially reviewed by one annotator. A random 600 packets (20% of the 3,000-packet set) receive a second review by the other annotator, without access to the first human answer. Pre-adjudication agreement on binary sufficiency is 82%. Of 108 disagreements, 36 are resolved through subsequent annotator review and 72 are adjudicated by the principal investigator. These labels concern the adequacy of the supplied evidence for deciding a claim, not the underlying veracity label. Support requires evidence for the material contextual assertions; refutation may follow from a decisive contradiction. Repeated accounts from a common source do not constitute independent corroboration. An empty external packet is not automatically insufficient when the image itself resolves a directly visible assertion. The diagnostic suite evaluates 600 base posts, with 200 from each benchmark, under nine conditions: clean evidence; evidence deletion at 25%, 50%, and 75%; removal of all external evidence; irrelevant evidence addition; hard distractors; conflicting evidence; and evidence pollution. All 5,400 resulting packets are evaluated and manually labeled for sufficiency, stance, key evidence, and conflict. Diagnostic packets are distinct from the 4,300 packets above, and their posts are disjoint from model training, tuning, and calibration. The 200 VERITE diagnostic posts nevertheless overlap with posts used for early annotation-rule development, because that resource covered the full VERITE benchmark. We use a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 representation backbone and a two-layer graph module with hid-den dimension 256 and dropout 0.1. Each example contains at most six atomic claims and five evidence groups. Claim extraction uses Qwen/Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct with greedy decoding (do sample=False ), an input limit of 4,096 tokens, and max new tokens=512 . Explanation generation uses the same input limit and decoding policy with max new tokens=256 .The graph module and prediction heads are optimized with AdamW at a learning rate of 10 −4 and weight decay 0.01. The effective batch size is 64, implemented as a single-device batch of 16 with four gradient-accumulation steps. Gradients are clipped at a global norm of 1.0. The graph module is trained in FP32; inference with the 7B vision–language model uses BF16 where supported. Training runs for at most ten epochs and stops early if the tuning loss does not improve for three consecutive epochs. The weights for veracity, stance, sufficiency, intervention, and Brier losses are 1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.3, and 0.1, respectively. The intervention ranking margin is δ = 0 .2. The random seeds are 42, 43, and 44. For locally run experiments, metrics are computed separately within each seed on the corresponding evaluation population and then averaged across seeds; predictions from different seeds are not concatenated. A single positive temperature is fitted by minimizing negative log-likelihood on the independent 300-packet calibration partition, separate from training and tuning. The fitted temperatures for seeds 42, 43, and 44 are 1.13, 1.08, and 1.17, respectively. A seed-42 pilot checkpoint, using the same temperature-calibration procedure, supplies predictions for joint threshold selection on the 300-packet tuning set. The grid spans τs ∈ [0 .3, 0.7] and τu ∈ [0 .2, 0.6] , both in steps of 0.02. Selection maximizes binary sufficiency F1 for the joint decision s ≥ τs and u ≤ τu against the sufficiency target a∗. The selected thresholds, τs = 0 .52 and τu = 0 .38 , are fixed for all three formal runs before test evaluation. Pilot threshold search, final rejection, and ranking all compute u from temperature-calibrated classification probabilities. Full-coverage evaluation reports accuracy and macro-F1 without removing rejected examples. SAFE-MR ranks examples by descending q = s(1 − u), with ties resolved by descending sufficiency probability s and then ascending original sample identifier. At target coverage c ∈ { 0.6, 0.8}, the leading ⌊cn ⌋ examples are accepted. Coverage-matched risks and AURC use this ranking rather than the fixed double-threshold acceptance set. Selective risk is the error fraction among accepted examples. Table 4 reports AURC, risk at 80% and 60% coverage, and two distinct ECE measures; AURC and ECE use the 0–1 scale, whereas risk is expressed as a percentage. For a ranked list with error indicators e(i), the discrete risk at coverage k/n is Rk = k−1 Pki=1 e(i). Discrete AURC is the arithmetic mean n−1 Pnk=1 Rk over all nonempty prefix coverages. # 6 DISCUSSION 

SAFE-MR is designed to expose an evidence boundary that a binary classifier usually hides. Its practical value would be a lower error rate at a fixed review workload, rather than a larger refusal rate in isolation. A verification service could route rejected examples to human reviewers with an explicit list of unresolved claims and missing provenance. Whether this improves reviewer efficiency requires a separate user study and is not established by offline metrics. The design also raises a distinction between epistemic restraint and excessive conservatism. A reliable archive can refute a contextual caption even when some secondary details are unknown. Requiring every fact to be covered before any decision would unnecessarily reject such cases. Conversely, numerous low-quality repetitions should not provide sufficient support merely through agreement. These asymmetries motivate learned sufficiency labels and provenance grouping rather than a single average-coverage threshold. The stress suite may overrepresent artificial evidence damage. For this reason, results must be paired with naturally incomplete packets and unseen events. A model trained on a particular pol-lution generator might recognize its style rather than learn verification. Generator-disjoint testing and paraphrase-preserving controls are needed before claiming robustness to synthetic evidence. Interventions remain local research fixtures; they are not published as apparently authentic news pages. 

# 7 LIMITATIONS 

The sufficiency target depends on judgments of claim materiality and evidence admissibility. Com-bined model and human annotation can retain systematic ambiguities or biases, particularly for conflicting accounts. Search-engine coverage and source-language availability can also disadvantage some claims. Domain reputation must not be equated with truth, and historical timestamps may be missing or incorrect. Pretrained models may have encountered benchmark claims or fact-checking pages. A downstream test split does not remove this possibility, and the present results do not establish temporal generaliza-tion or absence of pretraining contamination. The evaluation concerns static image–text verification; video manipulation, propagation modeling, and multilingual multi-image reasoning are outside its primary scope. Point estimates alone do not characterize run-to-run uncertainty. Synthetic evidence perturbations also approximate only part of the missingness and disagreement encountered in deployment. Explanation quality and the effect on human reviewer efficiency require separate evaluation. 

# 8 CONCLUSIONS 

We presented SAFE-MR, a selective multimodal verification framework that separates veracity prediction from evidence sufficiency. Atomic claims organize evidence, reliability-aware aggregation accounts for provenance, and intervention training distinguishes irrelevant changes from losses of informative evidence. Across the three classification benchmarks, SAFE-MR improves macro-F1 over the matched backbone with evidence. On the diagnostic set, it reduces AURC and coverage-matched error relative to confidence-based rejection and a learned selector. Ablations associate sufficiency learning primarily with better selection and intervention training with greater perturbation robustness. Together, these results support explicit evidence-sufficiency modeling as a useful complement to multimodal truth prediction. 10 Preprint

A.4 CALIBRATION , R ANKING , AND PERTURBATION RULES 

One positive temperature minimizes negative log-likelihood on the independent 300-packet calibration partition. Temperatures are 1.13, 1.08, and 1.17 for seeds 42, 43, and 44. The seed-42 pilot uses the same calibration procedure before joint threshold search on the separate 300-packet tuning set. The search grids are τs ∈ [0 .3, 0.7] and τu ∈ [0 .2, 0.6] with step 0.02. Binary sufficiency F1 of the joint threshold decision selects τs = 0 .52 and τu = 0 .38 , fixed for all three formal runs. Both final rejection and ranking use uncertainty from temperature-calibrated class probabilities. Ranking sorts q = s(1 − u) descending, then s descending, then original sample ID ascending. At coverage c, the first ⌊cn ⌋ samples are accepted. AURC is the arithmetic mean of risks over all nonempty ranked prefixes. Both ECE definitions use 15 equal-width bins but different targets. Classification-confidence ECE uses maximum class probability and classification correctness; sufficiency-probability ECE uses s

and binary sufficiency truth a∗. Each value is conditional on the corresponding selector’s accepted set. SAFE-MR’s ECE set satisfies s ≥ 0.52 and u ≤ 0.38 . These ECE populations are separate from the fixed-coverage sets used for risk reporting. The diagnostic design comprises 600 base posts, 200 per benchmark, evaluated in nine conditions to yield 5,400 packets. Clean packets retain the original evidence. Deletion operates on evidence items, removing ⌊rK ⌋ of the K original items for r ∈ { 0.25 , 0.50 , 0.75 }; the no-external-evidence condition removes all external evidence while preserving the post. Irrelevant additions introduce evidence unrelated to the material claim. Hard distractors are superficially relevant but do not resolve the claim. Conflict introduces incompatible accounts; pollution introduces misleading external evidence. Each construction preserves the original image–text post and veracity label. Irrelevant, hard-distractor, conflict, and pollution conditions each add one evidence group. After addition, if the packet contains more than five groups, the top five by relevance are retained. Thus, addition can displace an original group when the cap is reached. The particular deletion masks, distractor sources, and conflict/pollution content follow the perturbation records.
