## Atomic Visual Entailment: Enhancing Zero-Shot Vision–Language Reasoning through Atomic Fact Decomposition and Learned Selection

Abstract 

Visual entailment (VE) asks whether an im-age supports, contradicts, or leaves undecided a textual hypothesis. Strong results come from fine-tuning large vision–language models on labelled data, while zero-shot and hybrid ap-proaches remain far behind. A VE hypothesis often bundles several visual claims, yet exist-ing zero-shot methods reason over it as a single unit. We propose Atomic Visual Entailment 

(AVE), which decomposes the hypothesis into atomic facts, produces candidate predictions from both the full hypothesis and its facts us-ing frozen vision–language models, and pre-dicts the final label with a lightweight classifier trained only on how those candidates behave. We find that decomposition helps only when the hypothesis context is preserved: judging facts in isolation is worse than not decompos-ing at all. Full-hypothesis and atomic predic-tion make complementary errors, and learning which to trust recovers far more of that comple-mentarity than majority voting, reaching 0.803 test accuracy on SNLI-VE without fine-tuning any vision–language model. AVE also localises the visual evidence behind its prediction with-out region-level supervision. These results sug-gest that learning which candidate prediction to trust can close much of the gap to fine-tuned systems, offering a practical alternative where fine-tuning a vision-language model directly would need more labelled data or compute than is available.

Vision–language models (VLMs) now perform well across many multimodal tasks, including judg-ing whether an image supports a written claim. Visual entailment (VE) poses this as a three-way judgement over an image premise and a textual hypothesis: entailment, neutral, or contradiction (Xie et al., 2019). However, strong results on VE come from fine-tuning large VLMs on labelled data, and zero-shot and hybrid approaches remain comparatively unexplored. Recent zero-shot and hybrid work on VE adds intermediate reasoning steps between the image and the label. IdealGPT (You et al., 2023) gener-ates sub-questions for a VLM to answer in a zero-shot setting, and Inner Monologue Multi-Modal Optimization (IMMO) (Yang et al., 2024) trains a reasoner and an observer to alternate over several turns. In both, the intermediate steps interrogate the 1arXiv:2610.05630v1 [cs.CL] 4 Oct 2026 image rather than the claim. Yet a VE hypothesis often contains several visual claims, and reasoning over it as a single unit checks them all at once. A natural response is to decompose the hypoth-esis into atomic facts, so that each claim can be checked against the image separately. Hypothesis decomposition has been used in Natural Language Inference (NLI), the textual counterpart of VE, to inspect model decisions at the level of individual claims, and that work finds the global context still matters: claims are often only interpretable when read together (Stacey et al., 2024; Srikanth and Rudinger, 2025). Whether the same holds in VE, where each fact must be checked against an image rather than a textual premise, has not been studied. A second challenge is deciding among compet-ing predictions. A hypothesis can be judged as a whole or through its atomic facts, and instead of choosing one over the other, you can combine them to improve the prediction. Majority voting combines them without any training, but treats ev-ery candidate as equally reliable, so a tied or near-tied pool can be resolved in favour of a confidently wrong candidate (Figure 1). Related work on black-box ensembling instead learns which candidate to trust rather than counting votes, and does so with-out access to the models that produced the candi-dates (Xia et al., 2025). To address these two challenges, we propose 

Atomic Visual Entailment (AVE), a framework that decomposes the hypothesis into atomic facts and combines predictions from both views of the claim. AVE produces a pool of candidate predic-tions from two frozen VLMs under several predic-tion strategies and prompt styles. A lightweight classifier then predicts the final label from features describing how those candidates behaved. Because each candidate carries the reasoning that produced it, AVE also localises the visual evidence behind its prediction, without region-level supervision. On SNLI-VE (Xie et al., 2019), AVE outperforms ev-ery previously published zero-shot and hybrid re-sult, while every VLM remains frozen. In summary, our contributions are: (1) AVE, a framework that decomposes VE hypotheses into atomic facts and combines full-hypothesis and atomic predictions through a lightweight learned selector, without fine-tuning any VLM; (2) 0.803 test accuracy on SNLI-VE, 14.2 points above the strongest hybrid baseline and within the range of su-pervised systems; (3) Evidence that atomic reason-ing helps only with hypothesis context preserved, and that learned selection exploits the resulting complementary errors far better than majority vot-ing; (4) Zero-shot grounding of predicted evidence, reaching Recall@1 of 0.837 against human annota-tions without region-level supervision. 

2 Related Work 

Supervised visual entailment. VE was introduced together with SNLI-VE and the EVE model, which fuses image and hypothesis features to predict the three-way relation (Xie et al., 2019). Later work adapts large pretrained VLMs to the task, including UNITER (Chen et al., 2020) and OFA (Wang et al., 2022), which reports the strongest published accu-racy on SNLI-VE. These models require labelled SNLI-VE data and task-specific fine-tuning, which limits their use where such data is unavailable. 

Zero-shot and hybrid VE. Zero-shot ap-proaches avoid labelled VE data entirely. CLIP-based methods treat VE as image–text matching (Song et al., 2022), UniFine uses fine-grained vi-sual and textual cues (Sun et al., 2023), and Ide-alGPT decomposes the input into sub-questions answered by a VLM (You et al., 2023). Hybrid approaches combine zero-shot components with a small component trained on VE data: IMMO trains a reasoner and an observer to exchange questions and answers over several turns (Yang et al., 2024). 

Atomic decomposition. Decomposing a sen-tence into atomic claims has been studied for tex-tual NLI as a way to inspect model decisions at the level of individual claims. One direction de-composes the premise and derives an instance label from fact-level predictions (Stacey et al., 2024), while another decomposes the hypothesis to study whether models predict consistently across a claim and its parts (Srikanth and Rudinger, 2025). Atomic inference is harder than sentence-level in-ference, and requiring every atomic fact to be en-tailed does not hold reliably in practice (Huang, 2026). All of this work concerns textual premises. 

Black-box prediction selection. LLMs and VLMs are treated as black-box systems when their weights or confidence scores are unavailable, mak-ing fine-tuning difficult or costly (Xia et al., 2025). InfoSel addresses this by training a small selector to choose among candidate answers from several black-box models, using the question text, the can-didate answers, and image region features (Xia et al., 2025). Its selector therefore encodes the original inputs alongside the candidates, and its 2output is always one of them. AVE uses the same idea for VE, but its selector is trained only on how the candidate pool behaves and predicts the label directly. 

Evidence grounding. Fine-grained VE localises the visual evidence behind a prediction, but relies on region-level annotation collected for that pur-pose (Thomas et al., 2022). Open-vocabulary de-tectors localise arbitrary text queries without task-specific training (Liu et al., 2024), which removes the need for such annotation. 

3 Method 

In this section, we introduce the proposed AVE .Our focus is the task of VE, where a hypothesis H

is judged against an image I with a label from 

Y = {entailment , neutral , contradiction }. AVE consists of four components: a Decomposer , a 

Prediction Pool , a Selector , and a Grounder .Given H, the Decomposer splits the hypothesis into self-contained atomic facts A = {a1, . . . , a n},so that a claim bundling several assertions can be checked at a finer granularity (Sec. 3.1). The 

Prediction Pool applies several prediction strate-gies over H, A, and I using frozen VLMs, pro-ducing candidate predictions P = {c1, . . . , c K }

(Sec. 3.2). The objective is not to identify the sin-gle best strategy, but to obtain candidates that dis-agree in informative ways: at least one candidate is correct far more often than any individual strategy, which motivates treating the final decision as a se-lection problem. The Selector therefore predicts the final label ˆy by casting this decision as classi-fication over lightweight meta-features describing the candidates, their label likelihoods, and their agreement (Sec. 3.3). Finally, the Grounder lo-calises phrases from the VLM’s reasoning behind the selected candidate, tying the decision to visible evidence (Sec. 3.4). No VLM is fine-tuned at any stage. The overall pipeline is shown in Figure 2. 

3.1 Hypothesis Decomposition 

The Decomposer converts a hypothesis H into an atomic fact set A = {a1, . . . , a n}, where n varies with the number of distinct claims in H. If H

already expresses a single claim it is kept as one atomic fact; otherwise it is split into the fewest facts needed to cover its meaning. Each fact is self-contained, with unclear pronouns replaced by explicit noun phrases, and uses only information stated in H (Figure 11). We use Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct 1 as the 

Decomposer , selected from a comparison of text-only LLMs validated manually by an author on 100 sampled hypotheses (Appendix A). For each hypothesis, BM25 (Trotman et al., 2014) ranks a manually constructed pool of 50 hypothesis–atomic fact pairs by lexical similarity to H, and the four highest-ranked pairs are supplied as demonstrations. Decomposition is text-only and independent of the image, so A is computed once per hypothesis and reused by every strategy in the prediction pool.

3.3 Combining Candidate Predictions Majority voting. A training-free way to derive the final label from the prediction pool is majority voting (AVE-MV), which takes the most frequent generated label across its candidates, breaking ties by the largest score margin, the gap between a candidate’s top two label-likelihood scores from Equation 1. Each candidate’s vote counts the same regardless of how accurate that candidate is overall. 

Learned selection. At least one candidate in the prediction pool already matches the gold la-bel for over 94% of instances (Table 2), so the remaining problem is identifying which candidate to trust rather than re-examining the image or hy-pothesis. AVE-LS therefore casts the final decision as classification over meta-features describing how the pool behaved on a given input, rather than a model that also processes the image or text. For instance i, we form a feature vector xi whose fea-tures fall into four groups. Per-candidate features record each ck’s generated and score-based labels and its score vector ˜sk. From ˜sk we also derive the highest score, its margin over the second, and its entropy. Pool-level features summarise the label distribution across candidates, such as how often each label is predicted and whether more than half the candidates agree on one. Pairwise agreement 

features record whether each pair of candidates agrees, separately for generated and score-based la-bels. Decomposition metadata records the number 4of atomic facts and the lengths of H and A. The feature vector contains no image, no hypothesis or atom text, and none of the candidates’ justifications, making AVE-LS a meta-classifier over prediction-pool behaviour rather than over the original inputs. Tree-based classifiers handle this kind of mixed-scale tabular input directly, without the feature scal-ing or larger training sets that a neural classifier would need. We compare two tree-based classi-fiers, HistGradientBoosting and XGBoost, and se-lect XGBoost for its higher validation macro-F1; its hyperparameter configuration is given in Ap-pendix C. We train this lightweight classifier fϕ

(Chen and Guestrin, 2016) on the training split to predict the gold label from xi, giving ˆy = fϕ(xi)

Representative candidate. Predicting a label directly does not identify which candidate in P sup-ports it. When at least one candidate has generated label ˆygk = ˆ y, we select the one whose score for ˆy is highest and take its justification ek as the represen-tative output for that instance. Otherwise, the label is returned without a representative justification. 

3.4 Evidence Grounding 

AVE’s prediction includes a textual justification that describes the visual evidence but does not indi-cate where it appears in the image. The Grounder 

localises that evidence in two steps: it extracts a phrase from the justification ek of the representa-tive candidate, then locates that phrase in the image. Only entailment and contradiction predictions are grounded, since their justifications point to visible content that settles the claim. A neutral justification explains why the image leaves the claim undecided, so no region can be localised. Phrase extraction takes H, ˆy, and ek, and returns a short phrase naming visible content. For entail-ment, the phrase names content that supports the claim. For contradiction, it names what the image shows instead, since the contradicting evidence is what is visible rather than what H asserts. We use Qwen3-8B as the extractor (Figure 16). Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2024), using the grounding-dino-base checkpoint, then takes the im-age I and the phrase as a text query and returns bounding boxes for the matching regions. The boxes accompany the predicted label as its visual evidence, and ˆy itself is unchanged. 

4 Experiments 

We evaluate AVE on SNLI-VE. We first describe the setup, then report what the decomposition pro-duces and how the individual prediction strategies perform. We then compare majority voting with learned selection, both against the prediction pool itself and against prior work. Finally, we evaluate the grounded evidence against human annotations. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We evaluate on SNLI-VE (Xie et al., 2019), which pairs Flickr30k images with SNLI hypotheses labelled entailment, neutral, or contra-diction, and follows the official splits of 17,858 development and 17,901 test instances. Candi-date predictions are also generated for a 47,203-instance subset of the training split, which is used to train the Selector . Grounding is evaluated against Flickr30k Entities (Plummer et al., 2017), which provides human-annotated bounding boxes linking image regions to noun phrases in the correspond-ing captions. We sample 2,500 test instances per groundable label for grounding evaluation. 

Models. All models are used with greedy decod-ing, so repeated runs give identical output. Genera-tion is capped at 700 tokens, which accommodates the longest structured responses. Grounding DINO uses a box threshold of 0.35 and a text threshold of 0.25, and we retain the three highest-scoring boxes after suppressing near-duplicates. 

Selector training. AVE-LS is trained on the can-didate predictions for the training subset, holding out 20% for model selection. The classifier and its hyperparameters are chosen by validation macro-F1 (Appendix C). The development and test splits are used once, after this configuration is fixed. 

Metrics. We report accuracy and macro-F1 for label prediction. We additionally report an ora-cle upper bound: the proportion of instances for which at least one candidate in the pool matches the gold label, which is the accuracy an ideal selec-tor over that pool would reach. For grounding, we report Recall@1 and Recall@3 at an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, together with the mean best IoU among the three retained boxes. Since Flickr30k Entities annotates caption phrases, not hypothesis evidence, these scores measure lo-calisation quality but only approximate correctness. 

Compute. All experiments run on a university HPC cluster. The 32B Decomposer runs on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80GB memory. All 8B 5VLMs run on an NVIDIA A100 MIG instance with 40GB memory. The Selector trains on CPU, com-pleting in under two minutes per configuration.

4.6 Ablation Studies Independent atomic prediction. Judging each atomic fact in isolation and combining the per-fact labels by rule is the only strategy that performs worse than full-hypothesis prediction. It recovers 468 test instances where full-hypothesis prediction fails, but loses 644 where it succeeds (Figure 3a). Isolating a fact removes its context, and an error on any fact propagates to the final label with no recovery. The same pattern holds in textual NLI, where entailment of every atomic fact does not re-liably yield the sentence-level label (Huang, 2026). Label-level versus candidate-level selection. 

We compare predicting the label directly against selecting a candidate and inheriting its label. Using the same features, split and classifier, the label-level objective reaches 0.801 validation accuracy against 0.778 for the candidate-level objective. Choos-ing among twelve candidates that differ in model, prompt, and score is harder than choosing among three labels, so AVE-LS predicts the label directly. 

Prompt style sensitivity. Neither prompt style dominates, and the effect depends on both the model and the prediction strategy. For Qwen3-VL, the structured prompt helps atomic prediction (0.716 to 0.745) and self-decomposed prediction (0.709 to 0.748). For InternVL3, it hurts full-hypothesis prediction (0.752 to 0.723), and self-decomposed prediction with score-based labels col-lapses to 0.365 under the structured prompt while reaching 0.613 under the simple one. Since no style is reliably better, AVE keeps both in the pool and leaves the selector to weigh them. 

Hypothesis complexity. Accuracy declines for every strategy as the number of atomic facts grows, from 0.774 at one fact to 0.637 at four or more for full-hypothesis prediction (Figure 10). Part of this reflects label distribution rather than the strategies, since neutral grows more frequent at higher atom counts (Table 1) and remains hardest throughout. Independent atomic prediction falls behind from two facts onward, as isolated evalua-tion grows less reliable with more facts. Atomic and self-decomposed stay ahead of full-hypothesis up to three facts, after which atomic falls behind while self-decomposed remains strongest, though the four-or-more bucket holds under 1% of in-stances. 

5 Conclusion 

We study whether atomic hypothesis decompo-sition improves zero-shot visual entailment, and whether predictions from different reasoning routes can be combined. We propose AVE, which decom-poses the hypothesis into atomic facts, produces candidate predictions from both the full hypothe-sis and its facts using frozen VLMs, and predicts the final label with a lightweight classifier trained only on how those candidates behave. We find that decomposition helps only when the hypothe-sis context is preserved: judging facts in isolation performs worse than not decomposing at all. Full-hypothesis and atomic reasoning make complemen-tary errors, and learning which to trust recovers substantially more of that complementarity than majority voting, reaching 0.803 test accuracy on SNLI-VE without fine-tuning any VLM. Because the selected prediction carries the reasoning that produced it, AVE also localises the visual evidence behind that prediction, without region-level super-vision. 8Limitations 

Inference cost. AVE evaluates twelve candidates per instance, so VLM calls scale linearly with pool size, and adding models, prompt styles, or strate-gies increases this further. The selector itself adds negligible overhead. Reducing the pool while re-taining most of the benefit could reduce the infer-ence cost. 

Single dataset and language. All experiments use SNLI-VE, an English-language VE benchmark built on Flickr30k captions. Our conclusions there-fore apply to this evaluation setting. Validation across other entailment benchmarks, image do-mains, and languages is an important direction for future work. 

Approximate grounding evaluation. SNLI-VE has no annotations linking hypothesis-level evi-dence to image regions, so we evaluate against Flickr30k Entities, whose annotations describe cap-tion phrases rather than entailment evidence. Re-ported scores therefore approximate rather than directly measure grounding accuracy. A purpose-built annotation set is left to future work. 

Decomposition quality. Decomposition qual-ity was assessed manually by an author on 100 hypotheses, giving an initial estimate rather than a validated measure of faithfulness and complete-ness. A multi-annotator study could provide further validation. 

Selector transparency. AVE-LS’s per-instance decisions are not directly interpretable; its accuracy was still rising at the largest training size tested, and it is trained on the specific behaviour of the two VLMs and prompts used to build the pool, so it may not transfer if either changes. These aspects need further investigation. 

Ethics Statement 

SNLI-VE and Flickr30k Entities are publicly avail-able research benchmarks and involve no new data collection from human subjects. Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL3-8B are pretrained on large web cor-pora and may carry biases from that data into their predictions. Visual entailment is relevant to fact-checking and misinformation detection, where mis-placed trust in model outputs could have real-world consequences, so predictions should be used as a decision aid reviewed by a person rather than as an automated final judgement of whether a claim is true.
