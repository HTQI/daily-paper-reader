# VERITYGATE: A Four-Gate Schema-Level Faithfulness Framework and Paired Benchmark for Grounded LLM Narrations over Structured Evidence 

Sachin Gupta 

Independent Researcher San Jose, California, USA 

sachinkg12@gmail.com 

Abstract 

Fluent LLM explanations may not follow the evidence from a structured system. We present VERITYGATE, a four-gate checker for de-clared evidence IDs, entities, numbers, and claim types. It checks a fixed schema; it does not verify every fact in the prose. At r=0 

and r=1 , we test 900 instances per setting (450 grounded–ungrounded pairs) with GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3.3-70B, and Claude Sonnet 4.6. Under this schema-level contract and be-fore repair, 80.3% of mini claims and 47.9% of Sonnet claims fail. These are verifier rejec-tion rates, not prose-hallucination rates. One repair pass raises claim survival from 19.7% to 28.0% for mini and from 52.1% to 54.3% for Sonnet. Verified claims per example change by +0.14 for mini, -0.71 for Llama, and -0.47 for Sonnet, so survival and output volume must be reported together. A second Sonnet pass gives no clear gain. At r=1, Gate 4 covers 97.0%, 98.7%, and 100% of failing claims for mini, Llama, and Sonnet. Small human studies sup-port the rules but show gaps between schema checks and correct prose. A domain-specific GPT-4o judge test shows an order effect, so it is only a usefulness check. We release the code and data. 

1 Introduction 

The way users interact with structured decision sys-tems is changing. A user asks for a credit card recommendation, a treatment suggestion, or a loan risk assessment. A deterministic backend (a solver, a rules engine, a model) computes the answer. Then a large language model writes the explanation. This two-stage shape is increasingly common in tool-augmented and retrieval-augmented production sys-tems for delivering grounded explanations of struc-tured outputs. The convenience hides a problem. The LLM, even when given the full evidence graph from the backend, still makes things up. It cites cards that do not exist. It rounds numbers in ways that change their meaning. It flips signs. It invents facts that sound plausible. In production, this is not a research curiosity. In 

Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149) (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024), the air-line was held legally liable for false bereavement-fare information its support chatbot gave to a cus-tomer. In March 2024, New York City’s MyC-ity chatbot (a Microsoft-powered system for small business guidance) was found advising businesses to take a cut of workers’ tips and lock out tenants, both illegal (Lecher, 2024). In February 2025, three attorneys at a top-50 U.S. law firm were sanctioned for filing a motion in which eight of nine cited cases were AI-fabricated (Pazanowski, 2025). As of mid-2026, a public database of court rulings on AI-hallucinated filings has documented over 1,400 decisions (Charlotin, 2025). The cost of a hallucina-tion in a regulated sector is no longer hypothetical. Existing defenses are not enough. Safety align-ment within the model is trained against user-facing harms: jailbreaks, harmful content, direct prompt injection. It is not trained against “cite only the data I gave you.” Constrained decoding methods like Outlines and JSON schema mode force syn-tactic structure but do not check semantic correct-ness against an external backend. LLM-as-judge approaches can suffer position effects (our single judge chooses slot A in 83% of calls) and, in our di-agnostic, prefer fluent ungrounded narrations over verifier-emitted ones, despite having no access to the evidence graph. We present V ERITY GATE , a faithfulness evalua-tion framework for grounded LLM narrations, with three contributions: 1. A claim-level faithfulness taxonomy for grading LLM narrations against a structured backend’s evidence: evidence existence, en-tity allowlisting, number binding, and claim-

The taxonomy and verifier interface are designed to be domain-portable and apply to any pipeline where an LLM narrates over a structured backend that emits typed evidence with allowed entities and numbers; the evidence-graph builder and claim-type rules are domain-specific. 2. A paired benchmark (900 instances at each main setting, r=0 and r=1 : 50 profiles × 3goals × 3 narrators = 450 matched settings; grounded and ungrounded variants yield 900 instances) with pre-registered co-primary met-rics and paired bootstrap CIs on every contrast. We evaluate the pipeline on credit card portfo-lio optimization as a case study. The deterministic backend is a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) that computes the optimal 1–3 card portfolio for a user spending profile; the LLM narrator writes the explanation; V ERITY GATE sits between them. We choose MILP as the concrete instantiation be-cause it produces structured evidence with clean, audit-quality ground truth; the framework requires only that the backend emit typed evidence with al-lowed entities and numbers, a contract we revisit in Section 5. 1

We catalog the failure modes of LLM narrators when given full structured grounding. At each 

> 1The system’s name reflects its function: four mechanical gates (one for each failure mode in the taxonomy) through which every generated claim must pass before it ships to the user.

Gate 1: Evidence Existence. Every cited evi-dence identifier must exist in the structured back-end output. LLMs frequently fabricate identifiers that sound right but do not appear anywhere in the input. At r=0 (no repair) Sonnet 4.6 produces 1 evidence-existence error per 150 grounded in-stances and GPT -4 O-MINI produces 6; after one repair pass these drop to 0 and 4 respectively (Ta-ble 2). The check is deterministic: a set member-ship against the backend’s emitted identifier set. 

Gate 2: Entity Allowlisting. Cited entity names (card identifiers, category names, currency codes) must come from the deployment’s allowed entity set. LLMs paraphrase entity names from training prior, writing “Amex Blue Cash” when the backend used amex-bcp , or inventing “us-bank-altitude-go” when no such card was selected. The check is also deterministic: set membership against the back-end’s allowed-entity list. 

Gate 3: Number Binding. Cited number strings (dollar amounts, percentages, counts) must exactly match a canonical string surfaced from the struc-tured backend. LLMs round, sign-flip, fabricate, or change decimal formatting. At r=0 GPT -4 O-MINI 

produces 388 number errors across 150 grounded instances and Llama-70B produces 182; Sonnet produces 0 at r=0 and 1 at r=1 . These counts include exact-format mismatches such as 2400.0 

versus 2400.00 . Small integers (0–3) are allowed by convention. The evaluated check is exact string membership. 

Gate 4: Claim-Type Rules. Claims of a given type must cite evidence of the required type. A comparison claim must cite a winner-by-category evidence node; a fee-justification claim must cite a fee-break-even node; a cap-switch claim must cite cap-hit evidence and, when those node types are present in the graph, both cap-hit and allocation-segment evidence. LLMs frequently produce struc-turally well-formed claims that cite the wrong ev-idence category. This is the hardest gate in our evaluation: on Sonnet 4.6 the count drops only from 985 ( r=0 ) to 841 ( r=1 ) across 150 instances, and a second repair pass moves it no further (Sec-tion 4). 3 The V ERITY GATE Framework 

VERITY GATE operates as a transparent layer be-tween the deterministic backend and the LLM nar-rator. It requires no modification to either side. The backend emits structured evidence in its native format; the LLM is called with a standard prompt. The framework performs three-stage validation. 

3.1 Stage 1: Evidence Graph Construction 

We build an evidence graph from the backend out-put. Each evidence block becomes a node; edges connect logically related nodes (for example, a cap-hit node depends on the winner-by-category node for the same category). The graph also carries two allowlists: • Allowed entities: all entity strings that ap-pear in the backend output (card identifiers, category names, currency codes). • Allowed numbers: all numbers that appear in any evidence node’s structured fields, plus a small-integer allowlist (0, 1, 2, 3). The graph carries a SHA-256 digest of its canon-ical form. This makes the verification step deter-ministic and auditable: any two runs over the same backend output produce the same digest. 

3.2 Stage 2: Four-Gate Verification 

The narrator LLM is prompted (strict JSON) to emit prose plus a parallel list of structured claim objects with declared citedEvidenceIds ,

citedEntities , and citedNumbers (schema and Gate 4 type rules in Appendix C; prompt templates in Appendix D). The four gates check these de-clared structured fields against the evidence graph; the guarantee is schema-level structural correct-ness , not prose-level faithfulness: an under-citing LLM can mention unverified entities or numbers in prose that the verifier never inspects (Appendix A). Each claim is checked against the four gates from Section 2. The check is purely structural: no LLM is involved. If any claim fails any gate, the claim is rejected. Crucially, rejection is in-dependent of fluency. A grammatically beautiful, plausible-sounding claim that cites a non-existent number is rejected just like one containing an unal-lowed declared value. The output of Stage 2 is two lists: verified claims 

(passed all four gates) and failing claims (with attached error messages indicating which gate(s) failed and which strings were unallowed).

4 Evaluation 

We evaluate V ERITY GATE along three dimen-sions: claim-survival rate (the share of parsed-and-retained claims that pass verification), shipped verified claims per instance (the user-visible out-put), and judge-based usefulness preference (a sec-ondary metric).

4.3 Counterbalanced Judge Analysis 

We additionally evaluate user-facing usefulness via a counterbalanced LLM-as-judge protocol. The judge sees two explanations of the same optimiza-tion result: the verifier-emitted prose subset of the grounded narration (i.e., the user-visible output af-ter permissive emission) and the raw ungrounded narration, without knowing which is which and without access to the evidence graph. To control for position bias, every pair is judged twice with the order swapped. A pair score is computed by averaging the two ordering-wise grounded scores, producing one of {1.0, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.0} plus an inconsistent flag for pairs where the judge flipped its verdict across orderings. Across a deterministic stratified slice of 150 matched pairs (50 per narrator × 3 narrators, the first 150 of 450 in lexicographic (profileId, goal, modelId) order) = 300 judge calls, we observe: • 0% tie / parse-failure rate. Every call re-turned a definitive A or B vote with mean confidence 0.91. • 83% observed position-A rate. Across the 300 counterbalanced calls, the judge picks the explanation in slot A 83% of the time. • Mean preference score < 0.5 on all three narrators. Sonnet 0.30, mini 0.41, Llama 0.42: the judge prefers the ungrounded narra-tion across the board (a usefulness preference, not a faithfulness measurement; the judge sees no evidence graph). The 83% call-level position-A vote rate and 66% pair-level flip rate measure different quantities and are not algebraically interchangeable. Together they show a strong position effect while leaving room for content-sensitive decisions. Within the 34% of pairs where the judge agrees across order-ings, ungrounded wins decisively. 6 Related Work 

Structured-data and citation faithfulness. Slot-error methods check missing or spurious meaning-representation values (Wen et al., 2015; Dušek and Jurˇ cíˇ cek, 2016; Juraska et al., 2018; Nie et al., 2019; Dušek et al., 2019); table-to-text metrics use ex-traction or reference alignment (Wiseman et al., 2017; Dhingra et al., 2019); and ALCE checks ci-tations (Gao et al., 2023b). VERITY GATE adds a typed declaration/evidence contract and paired repair/filter evaluation, not a new set-membership operation (Appendix F). 

Other defenses and metrics. NLI and atomic-fact metrics cover less structured evidence (Es et al., 2024; Rashkin et al., 2023; Min et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023a; Yue et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). Complementary work studies constrained syntax (Willard and Louf, 2023), repair (Madaan et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024), judge bias (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), and tool safety (Radosevich and Halloran, 2025; Greshake et al., 2023). 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies hallucination in LLM-generated explanations of structured decisions. All experi-ments were conducted in controlled local environ-ments against a credit card portfolio benchmark. No production systems or production end users were involved. Three external raters provided of-fline labels for a small sample of generated claims; the released rater files contain no names or directly identifying information. No directly identifying personal data was processed: the benchmark pro-files used in this paper are generated parametrically; prior backend validation referenced in Section 4 used public-use BLS microdata, not personally identifiable records. We believe that document-ing the failure modes of grounded LLM narration (and providing a deployable defense) serves the public interest, since organizations cannot protect against risks they do not understand. Risk of false assurance from partial verification. A “verified” label produced by V ERITY GATE signals schema-level consistency between the declared claim fields and the evidence graph; it does not warrant that the prose shown to the user is faithful in full (see Appendix A). Deployments must surface this scope to end users (e.g., via product copy and/or audit logs) and must not present the label as a guaran-tee of complete factual accuracy. The versioned GitHub code release provides the framework imple-mentation; the prompts, schemas, pre-registration, and reported per-cell logs are archived in the ver-sioned Zenodo supplementary artifact under the repository’s permissive license. 

Limitations 

VERITY GATE has several limitations. First, we evaluate against three LLM backends. While these span a wide capability range (small / open-weights mid / frontier), the rapidly evolving landscape means results may not generalize to all future models. The observed pattern (repair helps the small model most while Gate 4 remains domi-nant in all three tested tiers) may not hold for other models; both the pattern and absolute numbers re-quire replication. Second, we use a single seed (42) for profile generation. We report tight paired bootstrap CIs as the primary uncertainty quantification rather than running multiple seeds. This is sufficient for the within-bench claims but does not assess seed ro-bustness. Third, our external rater studies (Appendix A) both use small stratified samples (3 raters × 25 items each, for Gates 1–3 and for Gate 4 sepa-rately). Larger studies with domain-expert raters and broader coverage are a clear follow-up. Note that the verifier’s primary check is against the de-terministic backend’s allowlists, not against human judgment; the rater studies probe a different ques-tion ( do humans, given the same allowlists, agree with the verifier’s accept/reject calls? ) rather than re-deriving the ground truth. Fourth, the LLM-as-judge analysis was con-ducted using a single judge model ( GPT -4 O). Other judge models may exhibit different position-bias magnitudes and different absolute preferences. We report the judge result as a secondary metric and explicitly tag it as “blinded pairwise usefulness preference under counterbalancing,” not as a faith-fulness measure. Fifth, adversarial robustness is not evaluated. The verifier is sound against non-adversarial LLM hallucination, but a model that knows the allowlist could craft outputs that pass verification while re-maining semantically misleading. This is out of scope for this paper, since our threat model is unin-tentional fabrication, not adversarial attack. Sixth, all empirical results are on a single appli-cation domain (credit-card portfolio optimization with the MILP backend described in §4). The veri-fier interface, claim schema, and Gate 4 type rules are designed to be portable, and we sketch map-pings to medical decision support and loan-risk assessment in Section 5; neither mapping is empiri-cally tested. Empirical cross-domain transfer is the most important follow-up. Seventh, we do not compare narrative quality or coverage against a deterministic evidence-graph template, ablate strict versus relaxed Gate 4 rules, or run NLI/RAGAS/ALCE scorers on the gener-ated outputs. The results therefore establish the behavior of the proposed structural audit-and-filter contract, not superiority over template generation or complementarity to neural faithfulness metrics.

Three independent raters with computer science backgrounds (no specific ML training) each rated the same stratified 25-item sample drawn from our paper-grade evaluation run: 10 items the verifier classified as VERIFIED and 15 items rejected by Gates 1–3. Appendix B. Rater Rubric 

Raters received the following one-page protocol prior to evaluation:       

> For each paired (claim, evidence) item, answer:
> Does this claim reference only entities and num-bers that appear in the evidence box? Choose one of: YES (every entity and number in the claim appears in the evidence box), NO (at least one entity or number is not in the evidence box), or
> UNCLEAR (the claim is too vague to extract en-tities/numbers, or an entity name is paraphrased and you cannot tell if it matches).

The rubric included five calibration examples covering YES, NO due to entity violation, NO due to number violation, UNCLEAR, and NO due to a derived (sum-of-allowed) number. Raters were instructed to be literal (do not use outside knowl-edge), to not perform arithmetic (derived numbers must appear directly in the evidence), and to trust their first read (mark UNCLEAR rather than debate for more than 60 seconds).

For citedEntities, use ONLY these exact strings (card ids, category names, currencies): {allowedEntitiesLine} For citedNumbers, use ONLY these exact strings (do not round, do not reformat; small ints 0–3 are also allowed): {allowedNumbersLine} Structured evidence (for reference): {evidenceJson} 

Ungrounded narrator prompt (skeleton). The ungrounded variant differs only in what evidence is supplied: 

Explain why this credit card portfolio is a good choice. Be specific. Cite card names, category earn rates, dollar amounts, and supporting reasons. {same CLAIM_SCHEMA block as grounded} Portfolio summary: {goal, portfolio card ids, primaryCurrency, netValueUsd, totalEarnValueUsd, totalCreditValueUsd, totalFeesUsd} 

The grounded variant gives the LLM the full evidence graph plus verbatim allowed-entity and allowed-number lists; the ungrounded variant gives only top-line breakdown numbers (net, earn, cred-its, fees) and the portfolio card list. Neither prompt instructs the LLM to “hallucinate” or “be careful”; the difference is entirely the evidence provided. 

Repair prompt (skeleton). On a repair pass, the original grounded prompt is reused with up to ten specific verifier errors appended: 

{original grounded prompt} Your previous output failed the claim verifier with these errors: - citedEntity not allowed: amex-gold - citedNumber not in allowed set: 4 - claim type COMPARISON requires citation of WINNER_BY_CATEGORY evidence (up to 10 errors shown) Produce a corrected JSON array. Cite only evidence ids, entities, and numbers listed in the prompt above. Use exactly the strings provided. 

Judge prompt (skeleton, used in §4.3). 

You are an impartial judge comparing two explanations of a credit card portfolio recommendation. Choose the explanation that is most useful to a user reading it. Consider: - Faithfulness to the context - Clarity - Helpfulness Do not let length or position influence you. Output ONLY a single JSON object on one line. Schema: {"winner": "A" | "B" | "TIE", "rationale": "<one short sentence>", "confidence": <float 0.0 to 1.0>} Context (the optimizer’s actual decision): {contextSummary: goal + portfolio ids only; no evidence graph} Explanation A: {prose A} Explanation B: {prose B} 

Note that the judge sees only the prose of the two explanations plus the (goal, portfolio) header. It has no access to the evidence graph, the verifier output, or which explanation is grounded; this is why we report the judge result as a usefulness-preference diagnostic rather than a faithfulness measurement. 

Sample evidence graph excerpt. A representa-tive grounded instance emits an evidence graph with the following shape (entity ids replaced with public-domain placeholders for the excerpt): 

{graphDigest: “sha256:5af1. . . ”, nodes: [ {id:“ev_1”, type:“ALLOCATION_SEGMENT”, card: “chase-freedom-flex”, category:“DINING”, spend:“1200.00”}, {id:“ev_2”, type:“WINNER_BY_CATEGORY”, category:“DINING”, winner: “chase-freedom-flex”, runnerUp:“amex-gold”}, {id:“ev_3”, type:“FEE_BREAK_EVEN”, card: “chase-sapphire-preferred”, fee:“95.00”, breakEven:“1267.00”}, . . . (typical instance has 14–22 nodes) ], allowedEntities: [chase-freedom-flex, chase-sapphire-preferred, DINING, GROCERIES, TRAVEL, . . . (30 strings)], allowedNumbers: [0, 1, 2, 3, 95.00, 197.64, 1031.00, 1200.00, 1267.00, . . . (typically 80–110 strings)] }

The graph digest is computed by canonicalizing the node list (sorted by id, fields in fixed order, all numbers as decimal strings) and hashing with SHA-256. The verifier’s accept/reject decision is a deterministic function of (claim, graph) and is reproducible across runs with bit-identical input. 

Appendix E. Pre-Registration Summary 

The released protocol/eval_preregistration. md separates these pre-run decisions from later anal-ysis and deployment choices: • Co-primary metrics: (a) claim survival =passing / totalClaims per cell, where the implemented totalClaims counts parsed-and-retained claims; (b) mean shipped verified claims per instance under permissive emis-sion. • Pairing units. Repair-effect contrasts: paired by (profileId, goal, modelId). Cross-model contrasts at r=1 : paired by (profileId, goal). Sonnet r=2 ablation: paired by (profileId, goal). • Statistical procedure. Instance-cluster boot-strap (10,000 iterations, seed 42); report paired differences with 95% CIs (significant iff excluding zero). • Run and decision rules. Compare r=0 

with r=1 ; claim a repair benefit only if a paired survival CI excludes zero. The pre-registered diminishing-returns rule used the Sonnet smoke r=2 − r=1 CI, which crossed zero at N =9 .• Stop conditions. Abort a run above 5% provider-call failures or 50% above its cost budget; diagnose before rerunning the af-fected cell. • Explicit non-claims committed up-front: 

– No claim of state-of-the-art on any open-domain factuality / RAG benchmark. 

– No claim of empirical cross-domain transfer or seed robustness. 

– No unqualified claim that grounding beats the ungrounded variant. 

Post-registration analysis and deployment choices. The per-instance best-of-( r=0 , r =1 ) de-ployment policy, the first-150 lexicographic judge sample, and the large-run Sonnet r=2 ablation were specified later.
