Abstract. The rapid progress of generative artificial intelligence has led to a significant increase in manipulated multimedia content, including synthetic faces, forged videos, AI-generated images, fake audio, and lo-cally edited images. Existing detection approaches are typically designed for a single manipulation type, limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios involving heterogeneous media sources. In this work, we present the Media Intelligence Platform (MIP), a unified web-based framework for multimodal media forensics integrating specialized modules for fake video detection, face morphing detection, AI-generated image detection, audio deepfake detection, localized image manipulation detection, and text analysis. Its modular architecture enables flexible integration of het-erogeneous models and supports future extensions for emerging manip-ulation techniques. Experimental results on representative benchmark datasets demonstrate reliable detection performance across multiple me-dia modalities while providing a practical and user-friendly interface for forensic investigations. The platform highlights the potential of unified multimodal systems for comprehensive deepfake detection in real-world environments, and is accompanied by a public demonstrator released with this workshop submission to support reproducibility and commu-nity evaluation. 

Keywords: Multimedia Forensics · Deepfakes · Disinformation Detec-tion 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly in-creased the realism and accessibility of synthetic media, including manipulated or fully generated images, videos, audio, and text. Powered by diffusion models and generative adversarial networks (GANs), such content can often be indistin-guishable from authentic media, creating new challenges for trust, security, and privacy. While these technologies offer creative and practical benefits, they also enable malicious applications such as disinformation, identity fraud, deepfake pornog-raphy, and harmful online content. However, most existing approaches focus on a single ma-nipulation type or media modality, often rely on dataset-specific training, and frequently lack transparency or practical deployment support. # 2 Related Work 

2.1 Multimodal Detection Systems 

Recent work has increasingly moved beyond single-modality detectors toward integrated verification platforms that combine multiple forensic models across images, videos, audio, and text. Browser-based systems such as InVID-WeVerify and the vera.ai Verification Plugin provide unified interfaces for media upload, Abbreviated paper title 3

URL analysis, and credibility assessment [49, 76]. Similarly, DeepFake-O-Meter v2.0 [33] aggregates multiple independent detectors for deepfake images, videos, and audio within a common online platform. Commercial systems such as True-Media [75], Sensity [68], and Reality Defender [64] extend this idea with API-based services for content moderation and fraud prevention. These systems demonstrate the growing relevance of multimodal media verification, but of-ten suffer from limited transparency, restricted deployment options, and closed evaluation protocols. 

2.2 AI-Generated Image Detection 

Detection of AI-generated images has evolved from artifact-based methods to-ward more generalizable deep learning approaches. Existing methods exploit semantic inconsistencies, spatial artifacts, or generator-specific fingerprints [7, 27, 86]. More recent work has shifted toward diffusion-generated images, which exhibit fewer visible artifacts and challenge traditional GAN-oriented detec-tors [14]. Explainable AI (XAI) methods such as Grad-CAM [67] and SHAP [43] are increasingly integrated to improve transparency and support human verifi-cation. 

2.3 Image Forgery Detection and Localization 

Image forgery detection and localization focuses on identifying manipulated re-gions in otherwise authentic images. Traditional methods addressed specific ma-nipulation types such as splicing [45], copy-move [39], and inpainting [38] using handcrafted forensic traces. Recent work (for an overview see [51]) increasingly relies on deep encoder–decoder architectures such as ManTra-Net [80] and U-Net variants [25], enabling joint detection and localization of manipulated content. These approaches provide both binary authenticity decisions and pixel-level lo-calization maps. 4 D. Fischinger et al. 

2.4 Face Morphing Attack Detection 

Face morphing attack detection is typically divided into single-image (S-MAD) and differential (D-MAD) settings [5]. Recent methods increasingly rely on deep learning, replacing handcrafted features with discriminative embedding models. In S-MAD, self-supervised and metric-learning-based approaches have shown strong performance [31,74], while D-MAD commonly employs Siamese architec-tures to compare probe and reference images [70]. 

2.5 Audio Deepfake Detection 

Audio deepfake detection can be divided into speech deepfake detection (DSD) and environmental sound deepfake detection (DESD). Recent methods increas-ingly leverage large pre-trained audio foundation models such as XLSR [54], WavLM [10], and BEATs [11], which are fine-tuned for downstream detection tasks. This transfer-learning paradigm has become dominant, consistently out-performing earlier handcrafted and task-specific approaches. 

2.6 Text-Based Detection 

Text-based disinformation detection increasingly combines automated classifica-tion with explainability and interactive analysis. Existing work addresses tasks such as fact-checking, propaganda detection, sentiment analysis, and rumor de-tection [50,69]. More recent approaches integrate linguistic, semantic, and stylis-tic features to improve interpretability and robustness [35, 37]. However, most systems remain limited to isolated tasks, motivating modular architectures that combine multiple text classifiers for toxicity, hate speech, and credibility assess-ment. Particularly relevant to online content moderation are toxicity and hate-speech detection systems, which identify abusive, offensive, or harmful language and are increasingly deployed as building blocks within larger trust-and-safety frameworks. 

# 3 System Architecture and Integration Framework 

The Media Intelligence Platform (MIP) is a modular, container-based frame-work for multimodal content analysis that exposes forensic and AI-based analy-sis components through a unified REST interface. The platform supports image and video forensics, audio analysis, and text classification, while remaining ex-tensible to future modalities and detection methods. Each analysis component is independently developed and deployed as a microservice, allowing the plat-form to scale without modifying the surrounding infrastructure. The complete system runs on an on-premises Kubernetes cluster and provides a reference web frontend (MIP-UI) for user interaction and access management. Abbreviated paper title 5 

> Fig. 3: Schematic of the MIP microservice architecture. Modules are deployed as Ku-bernetes pods and communicate via standardized REST APIs. Results and metadata are stored through a shared NFS file system and PostgreSQL backend.

Microservice Architecture Fig. 3 illustrates the overall architecture of MIP. We distinguish between Modules and Models . A Module represents a specific analysis task (e.g. AI-generated image detection or text classification), while a Model is the underlying neural network implementing the task. A single Module may en-capsulate multiple Models behind a common interface, enabling interchangeable inference without exposing internal implementation details. Each Module provides a standardized REST API for inference, status re-porting, and result retrieval. Inputs can be uploaded directly or referenced via shared storage. Results are returned through a common ModuleResult schema, allowing uniform storage and processing across all modules. Every Module is packaged as an isolated Docker container and can optionally provide custom UI components for configuration and result visualization. 

User Interface The microservice API is frontend-agnostic and allows arbitrary external applications to connect to the platform. We provide MIP-UI (Fig. 4) as a reference frontend, which implements user management, role-based access control, and job tracking. The frontend dynamically lists connected Modules, renders their configuration forms, and displays module-specific result visualiza-tions. Through its /modules endpoint, MIP-UI also allows external partners to build custom interfaces on top of the platform. 

Deployment MIP is deployed on on-premises hardware using Kubernetes and Helm-based infrastructure-as-code. The production system runs on multi-GPU hardware, while a separate development system supports internal testing. All services operate offline, which is essential for privacy-sensitive forensic workflows. To maximize hardware utilization, GPU resources are shared across multiple Modules using HAMi (Heterogeneous AI Computing Virtualization Middleware), enabling multiple inference services to run concurrently on a single GPU while maintaining memory isolation and fault containment. 

Design Principles The platform follows four core design principles: modularity ,

extensibility , reproducibility , and deployment independence . These principles al-6 D. Fischinger et al.

# 4 Visual Media Analysis Modules 

4.1 AI-Generated Image Detection 

The increasing realism of text-to-image generation models has created a growing demand for reliable AI-generated image detection. In particular, passive forensic detectors are essential when proactive methods such as watermarking are absent or can be circumvented [72, 85]. Within MIP, the AI-generated image detection module integrates four detec-tor variants and 16 XAI methods. The module accepts standard image formats and allows users to compare predictions across multiple models while selecting different XAI strategies for visual interpretation of the decision process. To improve robustness and generalization, we combine two architectural fam-ilies: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs), both of which have shown strong performance for AI-image detection [18, 87]. Specifically, the module integrates ResNet-50 [29] and ViT-B-16 [21] backbones with different fine-tuning strategies. While the ViT model is fully fine-tuned, the ResNet variants adapt progressively deeper layers. Training was performed on a mixed dataset of 162 k real images from MS-COCO [40] and 209 k synthetic images from the AIText2Image dataset [61] that has been generated with Stable Diffusion XL [60], DALL-E 2 [58] and Midjourney 5 [47]. Additional augmentations such as JPEG compression and common image filters were applied to improve robustness. Abbreviated paper title 7

4.2 Image Forgery Detection and Localization 

The Image Forgery Detection and Localization (IFDL) module is the primary image-forensic component of MIP and complements the AI-generated image de-tector by targeting classical image editing operations. In contrast to deepfake detectors, which mainly focus on synthetic faces or videos, IFDL addresses ma-nipulations such as splicing, copy-move, inpainting, object removal, and local content replacement. These manipulations remain highly relevant in real-world misinformation and fraud scenarios [28]. Traditional forensic methods rely on handcrafted traces such as JPEG ar-tifacts, sensor noise inconsistencies, or duplicated textures. While often inter-pretable, their robustness decreases under strong post-processing or recompres-sion. Recent work therefore increasingly relies on deep learning models that learn manipulation traces directly from data [51]. Within MIP, we integrate a hybrid U-Net–based convolutional network for joint manipulation detection and localization. The model employs a ConvNeXtV2-Base encoder pretrained on ImageNet and a U-Net–style decoder to generate high-resolution segmentation masks. In addition to pixel-level localization, a clas-sification head predicts a global authenticity score, enabling multi-task learning of both detection and localization. 4.3 Face Morphing Detection 

Face morphing combines the facial characteristics of two or more individuals into a single synthetic portrait. While originally developed for entertainment purposes, face morphing has become a serious security concern because mor-phed passport images can deceive face recognition systems and allow multiple individuals to authenticate against the same identity document [24]. This attack scenario, known as a face morphing attack , represents an important threat for border control, identity verification, and biometric security systems [4]. Recent morphing attack detection methods increasingly rely on deep learning, replacing handcrafted features with discriminative feature extractors pretrained on face recognition tasks. Within MIP, the deployed morphing attack detector is based on ArcFace [17] with a ResNet-100 backbone. ArcFace learns highly discriminative facial embeddings by enforcing angular separation between iden-tities, which makes it particularly suitable for detecting morphed images that contain blended identity information. For training, we curated a diverse morphing dataset from multiple passport-like face datasets and generated morphs using both classical landmark-based and recent deep learning–based morphing methods. This setup provides broad variability in acquisition conditions and morph generation techniques, improving generalization to unseen attacks. The model was fine-tuned for binary classification (bona fide vs. morphed) while preserving the discriminative facial representations learned during large-scale face recognition pretraining.

4.4 Face Swap Detection in Videos 

Face swap is a manipulation technique that transfers the face of a source person onto a target person in images or videos. When performed frame-wise using deep learning models, the resulting manipulated videos are commonly referred to as 

deepfakes . The most common manipulation categories include face swap, face reenactment, lip-sync, and full face synthesis. The deepfake video module in MIP focuses on detecting face swap and face reenactment manipulations. Given an input video, the system analyzes each detected face across sampled frames and assigns a manipulation score. The out-put is a per-person timeline of confidence values, enabling both frame-level and video-level forensic inspection. Rather than proposing a new detector, we integrate 19 state-of-the-art deepfake detection methods into a unified benchmark framework following DeepFakeBench [84]. This allows direct comparison under identical preprocessing and inference conditions. For evaluation, we curated a benchmark of 1000 videos (50 real and 50 fake from each of 10 datasets), enabling cross-dataset performance analysis. The benchmark results (Table 4) show that the strongest detectors are based on large pretrained vision backbones such as CLIP and hybrid CNN–Transformer architectures. The best-performing method, EFFORT, achieves a video AUC of 

0.928 , followed by Selimsef FbF (0.917) and GenConvViT (0.908). Our analysis revealed three important findings. 

Preprocessing quality. All detectors depend on accurate face detection and track-ing. Weak preprocessing pipelines frequently cause missed faces, incorrect iden-tity assignment, or unstable tracking, significantly degrading detection perfor-mance. By replacing the preprocessing stage with a unified GPU-accelerated pipeline, we improved runtime by up to 100 × for some detectors while increas-ing robustness across all integrated methods. 

Identity leakage. Cross-dataset generalization remains a major challenge. As shown in [20], many detectors implicitly learn facial identities instead of manip-ulation artifacts, causing strong in-dataset performance but poor transferability. Identity-disjoint train/test splits are therefore essential for reliable evaluation. 

Foundation model adaptation. The best-performing methods consistently rely on large pretrained vision models and lightweight fine-tuning strategies such as LoRA, SVD-based adaptation, or partial layer unfreezing. These approaches preserve general visual representations while adapting to forensic artifacts. Abbreviated paper title 11

# 5 Audio Deepfake Detection 

Audio deepfake detection has become increasingly important with the rapid progress of neural speech synthesis and voice cloning models. Synthetic speech can be used for impersonation, fraud, and disinformation, making robust forensic detection essential. The Media Intelligence Platform provides a dedicated Deepfake Speech De-tection (DSD) module for analyzing manipulated speech recordings. Following recent trends in the literature, the module is based on a fine-tuned pre-trained audio foundation model. 

Model Architecture. The DSD module uses the pretrained WavLM backbone [10] to extract feature embeddings from 4-second audio segments. Training follows a three-stage optimization strategy (see Fig. 7). First, multiple supervisory ob-jectives are applied jointly, including A-Softmax loss for multi-class spoof clas-sification, Contrastive Loss to separate bona fide and spoofed embeddings, and Center Loss to compact bona fide embeddings. In the second stage, the model is fine-tuned for binary classification. In the final stage, the backbone is frozen and bona fide embeddings are used to estimate a Mahalanobis distribution, enabling anomaly-based inference for unseen spoofing attacks. 

Training and Evaluation. The model was trained on ASVspoof 2019 (LA) [77], ASVspoof 2021 (LA & DF) [81], and ASVspoof5 [1]. Evaluation was performed on three unseen datasets: In-The-Wild [52], ASVspoof5 Evaluation Subset [1], and FakeAVCeleb [34], allowing assessment of cross-dataset generalization.

fests through multiple linguistic, semantic, stylistic, and ideological signals. To address this, MIP integrates a modular text analysis framework that combines several specialized classifiers for detecting harmful or manipulative communica-tion patterns. 

Modular Classification Framework. The framework is built around transformer-based language models, primarily XLM-RoBERTa (XLM-R) [13], fine-tuned for a range of classification tasks on German news and social media datasets. The implemented modules cover hate speech, toxicity, sexism, extremism, offensive language, sentiment, reporting style, and writing style detection. To improve interpretability and robustness, transformer embeddings can be combined with handcrafted linguistic features such as punctuation patterns, sen-timent scores, stylistic indicators, and structural text properties. These features complement contextual embeddings by capturing rhetorical and surface-level signals relevant to manipulative communication. Experimental results show that transformer-based models perform strongly on standard classification tasks, while fusion architectures improve robustness in multilabel and long-document settings. 

Disinformation Signals. Rather than directly classifying content as “fake” or “real”, the framework focuses on identifying indicators commonly associated with disinformation and manipulation. These include propaganda framing, clickbait, sensationalism, polarization, subjective writing, toxicity, and ideological extrem-ism. Such signals often become more informative when analyzed jointly rather than in isolation. 

Rule-Based Aggregation. Classifier outputs are aggregated into four reliability levels ( Highly Questionable , Questionable , Slightly Questionable , Reliable ) using 14 D. Fischinger et al. 

weighted rules based on indicator severity and co-occurrence, providing an inter-pretable overall assessment. This aggregation combines detector outputs using weighted rules based on the severity and co-occurrence of individual indicators, allowing a more transparent and explainable reliability assessment. 

MIP Integration. Within MIP, users can run individual text classifiers inde-pendently or combine them into a unified disinformation analysis pipeline. This modular setup supports applications such as content moderation, propaganda analysis, journalism support, and credibility assessment, extending the platform beyond multimedia forensics toward broader multimodal media intelligence.

# 8 Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented the Media Intelligence Platform (MIP), a unified and modular framework for multimodal media forensics. Unlike most existing approaches that focus on a single manipulation type or modality, MIP inte-grates state-of-the-art analysis modules for AI-generated images, image forgery localization, face morphing detection, deepfake video and audio detection, and text-based content analysis within a common microservice-based architecture. The proposed design enables scalable deployment, standardized access through REST APIs, and flexible integration of heterogeneous forensic models. By com-bining multiple complementary analysis modules, MIP reduces tool fragmenta-tion and supports practical forensic workflows involving images, videos, audio, and text. Experimental evaluations across representative benchmark datasets demonstrate that the integrated modules achieve strong performance across their respective tasks. The public demonstrator further provides researchers and prac-titioners with direct access to the platform for experimentation and validation. Future work will focus on extending the platform to emerging manipula-tion techniques, improving robustness against unseen generative models, and strengthening multimodal reasoning across heterogeneous forensic evidence.
