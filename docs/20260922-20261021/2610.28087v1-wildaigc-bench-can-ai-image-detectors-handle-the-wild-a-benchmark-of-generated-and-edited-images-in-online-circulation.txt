# WildAIGC-Bench: Can AI Image Detectors Handle the Wild? A Benchmark of Generated and Edited Images in Online Circulation 

Zijian Yu 1, Hangwei Zhang 1, Jun Lan 1,* ,Huijia Zhu 1, Weiqiang Wang 1, Jianfu Zhang 21Ant Group 2Shanghai Jiao Tong University Contact: yuzijian.yzj@antgroup.com , yelan.lj@antgroup.com 

Abstract 

AI-generated images linked to news events and online claims can mislead audiences, enable fraud, or cause public controversy. We introduce WildAIGC-Bench , built through recurring collection across news websites, fact-checking sources, and related social media content. The pipeline combines broad coverage, timely updates, and human-verified labels through scheduled discovery, automatic screening and preliminary annotation, and human review of image-specific evidence. The benchmark contains 407 verified AI-generated or edited images, each accompanied by a source webpage link and an evidence summary. Images retain the conditions encountered on-line, including compression, screenshots, and recapture. Evaluation of dedicated visual detectors, multimodal large language models, and a simple Perception Encoder baseline reveals substantial recall gaps relative to existing benchmarks. WildAIGC-Bench provides both a practical detection challenge and a basis for continued data expansion and source-based verification. The dataset is released at https://github.com/YuZijian/WildAIGC-Bench .

# 1 Introduction 

Advances in image generation and instruction-based editing make it increasingly easy to fabricate images that resemble ordinary photographs [1, 2]. When circulated in connection with news events, these images can become apparent evidence for misleading claims about people, actions, or public incidents. Even a local edit can change a scene’s meaning while preserving much of its photographic appearance. This creates a practical need for AI-generated content (AIGC) detection in everyday information flows, where plausible images may mislead users, enable fraud, or cause harmful public controversy. Existing detection methods broadly fall into two groups. Dedicated visual detectors learn generation artifacts or use pretrained image representations for classification [3, 4]. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) combine visual analysis with language-based reasoning to predict authenticity and explain their decisions [5, 6]. Benchmarks evaluate these methods across generators and image categories, as well as under image degradation [7, 8]. In-the-wild benchmarks further include images from creative communities and social networks, or examine the effects of actual platform transmission [4, 9–11]. Existing collections address diverse generation workflows and sharing conditions. A complemen-tary concern is whether detectors recognize photographic forgeries encountered in news reports and

Evaluating this setting requires finding relevant images as they circulate, establishing evidence of AI involvement, and retaining their acquired condition. A construction process that can continue discovering candidates is therefore valuable alongside the benchmark itself. We introduce WildAIGC-Bench to evaluate detection on photographic-looking AI imagery circulating around news events and online claims. Its construction follows reports across news websites, fact-checking sources, and related social media content, bringing together diverse events and forms of generation and editing. Scheduled tasks continually discover, screen, and preliminarily annotate new candidates as they circulate. Human reviewers then verify the labels against source evidence and decide which images to include; no AI-image detectors are used for selection. This workflow enables continued expansion while preserving a documented basis for each label. Images retain the compression, screenshots, recapture, and other conditions encountered online. Subsequent releases will broaden event coverage and add real-image controls, including original photographs underlying some AI recreations, enhancements, and edits. We evaluate dedicated visual detectors, including PE-Det, a simple Perception Encoder reference baseline, alongside MLLMs [12]. Every evaluated configuration has lower recall on WildAIGC-Bench than its external mean. The highest recall on processed images is 60.93%, leaving over one-third of the images undetected. Higher recall from some MLLMs is accompanied by elevated false-positive rates on real images in the external benchmarks, limiting their overall detection advantage. Our main contributions are: 1. A continuously operating construction pipeline. The pipeline delivers broad coverage, timely updates, and human-verified labels by combining recurring collection across sources with automatic screening and preliminary annotation, followed by human verification and quality control. 2. A benchmark of real-world news-related AI imagery. WildAIGC-Bench captures photographic-looking AI-generated and edited images circulating around news events and online claims, preserving real sharing conditions with human-verified labels and documented source evidence. 3. An analysis of detector limitations in real-world settings. Cross-benchmark evaluation reveals consistent recall gaps for visual detectors and MLLMs. Paired crop analysis and shared misses characterize challenges across image content, editing, and circulation conditions. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 AIGC Detection 

Dedicated visual detectors approach AIGC detection through several complementary direc-tions. Artifact-based learning captures transferable generation traces through augmentation or reconstruction-based contrastive training [13, 14]. Representation-based approaches use pretrained features or combine semantic and low-level cues for classification [3, 4]. Data-centric approaches align the content and processing characteristics of real and synthetic training images, reducing shortcuts unrelated to generation [15, 16]. MLLM-based detection combines authenticity prediction with language-based analysis. Explanation-supervised approaches learn from artifact descriptions to connect predictions with visual evidence [17]. Reasoning-oriented approaches use structured supervision or reinforcement learning to develop evidence assessment, including planning, self-reflection, and reasoning across image and video inputs [5, 18]. Perception-oriented approaches strengthen sensitivity to fine details, 2semantic anomalies, and pixel-level differences, then integrate these abilities into reasoning through distillation [6]. WildAIGC-Bench tests whether these visual and reasoning capabilities transfer to verified forgeries from real news events. 

2.2 AIGC Detection Benchmarks 

CNNDetection [13] and UnivFD [3] support evaluation across generation architectures. GenImage provides large-scale cross-generator and degradation tests [7]; Synthbuster focuses on diffusion imagery [19]; and EvalGEN includes newer generation architectures [16]. WildFake supports training and generalization analysis across diverse content and hierarchically organized generator types, architectures, weights, and versions [8]. AIGIBench broadens task coverage and includes social-network and creative-community content [20]. Many standard evaluations rely on clean generator outputs. Their results may not reflect detection after compression, screenshots, and recapture in real use; predefined degradation tests cover only selected aspects of these conditions. 

2.3 In-the-Wild Benchmarks 

In-the-wild benchmarks target real-use conditions through online image collection or realistic sharing processes. Chameleon emphasizes realistic content from ArtStation, Civitai, and Liblib [4]. SynthWildX collects images from X [21], while WildRF uses social-network imagery [9]. ITW-SM covers multiple social platforms, obtaining AI images from accounts and communities that openly share generated content [10]. MIRAGE includes online news, social media, and e-commerce scenarios alongside pipelines that combine multiple generation steps [22]. RRDataset examines actual platform transmission and screen capture and recapture [11]. Creator and AI-community collections can favor works showcasing generation capabilities, whose content may differ from everyday photographs despite appearing realistic. Simulated degradation and controlled sharing or recapture tests cover selected conditions rather than the varied histories of images already circulating online. WildAIGC-Bench instead follows actual news events and misleading claims to collect photographic forgeries, preserving their acquired condition. Every sample is manually verified and accompanied by a source link and AI evidence, connecting detection evaluation to documented real-world forgery scenarios. 

# 3 WildAIGC-Bench: Construction and Continued Expansion 

3.1 Automatic Collection, Screening, and Annotation 

The pipeline achieves broad coverage by following news reports, fact-checking websites, and related social media content across public figures, social affairs, disasters, and other widely discussed events (Figure 1). Scheduled tasks support timely updates by collecting and screening newly circulating candidates. They prepare preliminary annotations from source descriptions, creator disclosures, watermark evidence, and fact-checking findings. Human review checks these annotations before inclusion, producing human-verified labels for each release. The collection targets photographic-looking images tied to news events and online claims, including fabricated scenes and local edits that may mislead audiences, enable fraud, or cause public controversy. 3.2 Human Annotation Verification and Quality Control 

Human reviewers check whether each automatic annotation is correct and perform sample-level quality control to decide inclusion. Review checks whether the available evidence establishes AI generation or editing of the target image, rather than merely a false claim about an event. Accepted evidence includes creator disclosures, visible platform watermarks, and explicit fact-checking findings. Invisible-watermark evidence comes from detection results publicly reported by fact-checking organizations; our team does not independently rerun those tools. Candidates with insufficient evidence or uncertain AI involvement are excluded. Human review also rejects obviously stylized artwork and unusable images while retaining the degradations encountered during real circulation. No AI-image detectors are used for sample selection. Each accepted sample is accompanied by a source webpage link and an evidence summary, making the basis for its label inspectable. Additional annotation details and an example appear in Appendix A. 

3.3 Versioned Release and Dataset Profile 

The current v1 release contains 407 AI-generated or edited images . New candidates undergo human verification and quality control before entering a subsequent release; evaluation results remain tied to a fixed version. We will release expanded versions with more samples and broader event and source coverage, and add real images, including original photographs corresponding to some AI-recreated, enhanced, or edited samples. Each sample includes an acquired version ( raw ) and an evaluation version ( processed ). Where appropriate, a rectangular crop removes surrounding interface elements, comments, or explicit AI cues while retaining the target content; otherwise the image is unchanged. Preparation introduces no resizing, inpainting, or enhancement. There are 83 cropped images and 324 unchanged pairs; both versions are evaluated. Their acquired condition preserves the effects of online sharing, including compression, screenshots, and recapture. Figure 3 summarizes the dataset profile. Median short-edge lengths are 716 pixels for raw and 700 pixels for processed images. Linked source pages span 78 website hostnames, covering diverse news and fact-checking outlets. # 4 Experiments and Analysis 

4.1 Evaluation Setup 

We evaluate DRCT [14], DDA [16], AIDE [4], B-Free [15], Effort [23], PGC [24], OmniAID [25], and CO-SPY [26] as dedicated visual detectors. The MLLMs include specialized detectors—FakeVLM [17], BusterX++ [18], and Veritas++ [6]—and general-purpose models: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [27], Qwen3.8-27B [28], and GLM-5.3-Flash [29]. Together with PE-336 and PE-518, all sixteen configurations cover the external benchmarks and WildAIGC-Bench. Open-source methods use public code and weights, with each method’s preprocessing and decision settings held consistent across benchmarks. Qwen3.6, Qwen3.8, and GLM use the same prompts as Veritas++. 

PE-Det baseline. Recent studies have reported advantages of PE representations for AI-image detection [30, 31]. We fine-tune PE-Core-L/14 [12] with a two-layer classification head and focal loss [32]. 4.2 Performance Across Benchmarks 

Table 1 contrasts external performance with WildAIGC-Bench. PE-518 achieves the highest external mean recall, 93.51%, and lowest mean FPR, 2.70%. MLLM recall should be interpreted together with false positives: FakeVLM reaches 90.63% external mean recall with 45.30% mean FPR, whereas Veritas++ reaches 82.86% recall with 19.72% FPR. All evaluated configurations have lower WildAIGC-Bench recall than their external mean. The recall drops range from 21.87 to 70.41 percentage points. 

4.3 WildAIGC-Bench and Transfer Gaps 

On processed inputs, recall ranges from 13.02% to 60.93%. GLM-5.3-Flash achieves the highest recall but still misses over one-third of the AI images. Qwen3.8 reaches 52.83%, compared with Qwen3.6’s 35.63%. PE-518 drops from 88.40% recall on ITW-SM to 23.10% here, a 65.30-point gap; BusterX++ drops from 94.22% to 29.48%. These gaps show limited transfer to forgeries found through real events. Increasing the PE input resolution from 336 ×336 to 518 ×518 improves external mean BAcc from 92.3% to 95.0% and WildAIGC-Bench processed recall from 20.15% to 23.10%. This comparison supports the benefit of higher input resolution for PE-Det, although it does not close the transfer gap. 4.4 Shared Detector Blind Spots 

On processed inputs, 46.93% of images are missed by at least three-quarters of the evaluated detector configurations. Reviewing this subset and its source records reveals several shared characteristics. Scenes involving people recur throughout this subset, including posed group photographs, social gatherings, public appearances, sports, and law-enforcement activities. Other samples depict disasters, damaged infrastructure, conflict, and animal rescue. Their presentation resembles familiar forms of visual reporting: news photographs, personal snapshots, historical photographs, broadcast stills, and surveillance views. The subset spans both fabricated scenes and changes to existing photographs. Source records describe altered people, expressions, clothing, flags, and signs, as well as AI reconstruction of details from blurred footage. Visual quality also varies, from clear portraits and group photographs to low-light, blurred, or screenshot-based images. Shared misses therefore extend across content types, the extent of modification, and image quality levels, rather than being confined to visibly degraded inputs. 

# 5 Limitations and Future Work 

The current release is modest in size and focuses on forgeries collected through news and fact-checking contexts. It characterizes a challenging subset rather than the full distribution of online images, and currently lacks real-image controls. The planned expansion described in Section 3 will support paired comparisons, false-positive evaluation, and broader assessment of detection performance. The reported results apply to the evaluated configurations. Image content and sharing conditions jointly contribute to the observed difficulty; we do not reconstruct each image’s complete sharing history or isolate individual degradations. We also lack a user study comparing detector performance and error patterns with human recognition. The source links and evidence summaries are unused in the current image-only evaluation. Future work will explore agents that actively search for relevant source evidence and combine it with visual analysis to verify image authenticity in real-world online settings. 

# 6 Conclusion 

WildAIGC-Bench combines a continuously operating collection pipeline with a benchmark of photographic-looking AI imagery from news events and online claims. Automatic collection, screening, and preliminary annotation are followed by human annotation verification and quality control for final inclusion. Continued collection will support broader releases with more AI images and real-image controls. All evaluated configurations have lower recall than their external mean; the highest processed recall is 60.93%. # A Construction Workflow and Annotation Details 

The construction pipeline assigns collection, initial screening, and preliminary annotation to scheduled automatic tasks that are already running. Human reviewers then verify whether the automatic annotations are correct and perform quality control to decide whether each sample enters the benchmark. Automatic annotations remain provisional until this review is complete. 

Image-specific evidence. To verify an automatic annotation, reviewers check that the supporting evidence concerns the target image and establishes AI generation or editing. A false event claim alone is insufficient: a genuine photograph may also be shared with an incorrect description. Creator disclosures, visible platform watermarks, and explicit fact-checking findings can support admission. Invisible-watermark findings are taken from publicly reported fact-checking results rather than independent tool runs by the team. Candidates with insufficient evidence or uncertain AI involvement are excluded. 

Quality control and image preparation. Human reviewers make the final inclusion decision, excluding obviously stylized artwork and unusable images while retaining real circulation degra-dations. Accepted samples preserve their acquired image and supporting source information. A target-region crop removes peripheral text and interface elements where appropriate, while the raw image remains available for paired evaluation. No AI-image detectors are used for sample selection. 

Verification and cropping example. Figure A1(b) shows the purported image of Imran Khan arriving at a hospital. Soch Fact Check reports a positive SynthID detection, supporting the AI label. The acquired screenshot also contains the uploader’s explicit AI-enhancement disclosure and a comment calling the image fake. MLLMs can read these peripheral statements and potentially infer the label without detecting manipulation in the target image, giving them an advantage over visual detectors that focus on image features. Cropping removes these direct textual clues and supports a more comparable assessment of the target content. Source evidence remains available for annotation verification but is not supplied separately to detectors. 

Continued collection and releases. The active pipeline continues to supply candidates while the current evaluation set remains fixed. Later releases will incorporate newly admitted samples and broaden coverage of events and information sources. Planned real-image additions include, but are not limited to, original photographs from which some AI-recreated, enhanced, or edited images were derived.
