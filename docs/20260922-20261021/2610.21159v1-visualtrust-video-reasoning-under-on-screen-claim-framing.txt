## ABSTRACT 

On-screen text can describe what a video shows, or it can frame what the video is supposed to mean. Current benchmarks treat both alike, testing whether a model can read the text rather than whether it should trust it. We study claim deference ,multimodal large language models’ tendency to let an on-screen claim override conflicting visual evidence. We introduce VisualTrust , a counterfactual bench-mark with 5,063 cases across 14 claim-fact classes. Each case pairs a question with three versions of the same video: one with no claim, one with an accurate claim, and one with a misleading claim that alters a single grounded fact. The op-tion the misleading claim supports is the trap answer . Across seven open-weight and four proprietary models, mean accuracy falls from 90.1% without a claim to 51.9% with a misleading claim. Among initially correct responses, 42.8% flip to the trap answer and only 0.6% become other errors. Strong no-claim accuracy does not ensure resistance. We also introduce DEAR (Decoupled Evidence Ar-bitration and Reading ), a training-free agent for video question answering that separates evidence arbitration from reading. An independent check reports con-flicting evidence, a selector weighs it without a fixed modality priority and blinds what it withholds, and the reader answers from the resulting video in a fresh con-text. On 500 videos across three proprietary models, DEAR raises accuracy under misleading claims from 47.2–64.0% to 81.8–85.2% and reduces trap-answer se-lection from 34.4–50.8% to 11.6–15.0%, while preserving no-claim accuracy. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Modern video feeds are designed to be consumed quickly: clips arrive one after another, so creators have only a few seconds to shape how viewers interpret what they see. On platforms such as TikTok, YouTube Shorts, and Instagram Reels, videos reach viewers with text already on screen. Some of it is scene text , recorded by the camera as part of the environment. Some is added by the creator to transcribe speech or mark a channel. And some is claim-bearing text : words rendered over the video that frame or assert something about its content. A few such words can tell viewers what they are about to see before the video has a chance to speak for itself. They may faithfully summarize the scene, but they can also exaggerate, misrepresent, or contradict what the visual evidence supports. At platform scale, these textual claims are a routine part of how videos are made and consumed. These claims are also part of the input a multimodal large language model (MLLM) receives. Recent benchmarks have pushed video MLLMs in two directions. Some evaluate whether models under-stand video content across temporal, spatial, and semantic dimensions (Fu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). Others evaluate whether models can recognize, track, and reason over on-screen text of every kind, scene text and overlays alike (Shi et al., 2025). Yet reading is not the same as trusting (Fig-ure 1). The difference bites hardest for claim-bearing text, because a street sign the camera records

Standard benchmarks do not make the distinction. They treat all on-screen text as content to extract or context to use, so a model may read a claim correctly and still fail by accepting it as fact when the video does not support it (Deng et al., 2025). This matters for content moderation, retrieval, and summarization, where an unsupported on-screen claim can become an unsupported model output. We call this failure mode claim deference . It is distinct from object hallucination, which arises from internal priors (Li et al., 2023), and from sycophancy, which arises from conversational pres-sure (Sharma et al., 2024). In our setting, the misleading signal is part of the visual input, pixels the model is simultaneously asked to read and to ground against what the camera recorded. We formulate this problem as video reasoning under on-screen claim framing . At its core is a question of modality arbitration. When the rendered words and the recorded scene disagree, which does the model trust? For a question about a visually observable event, a grounded response should remain consistent with the relevant visual evidence. What we test is not whether models can resist text injected to attack them, but how they weigh a claim channel that real videos already carry. To isolate claim deference from ordinary visual error, we construct controlled counterfactuals. For the same underlying video and question, we compare model behavior when the rendered claim is removed, replaced with an accurate claim, or replaced with a misleading one. We instantiate this design in VisualTrust , a controlled counterfactual benchmark built over five video sources that span short creator clips to hour-long videos. Each misleading claim is derived from its accurate counterpart by a grounded single-point edit to a fact directly observable in the clip, with the correct answer and trap answer fixed before either claim is written. The paired claims are rendered onto the same base video with shared typography, placement, and timing, so the two claim conditions differ only in the fact they assert. Its 5,063 cases fall into 14 claim-fact classes, covering entities, attributes, events, relations, and other visually grounded propositions. Our experiments compare seven open-weight and four proprietary video MLLMs. Mean accuracy is 90.1% with no claim, 98.5% with an accurate claim, and 51.9% with a misleading claim. More importantly, 42.8% of initially correct responses flip to the trap answer and only 0.6% move to 2another error. This concentration identifies claim-directed reversals rather than a general loss of accuracy. Strong no-claim performance does not ensure resistance. Responses flip to the trap answer across all 14 fact classes. Complementary reasoning traces reveal claim anchoring : models cite the rendered claim as grounds for a response and can explain away conflicting evidence to preserve it (Section 5.4). These traces reflect stated justifications, not the model’s internal process. A trace can note an inconsistency and still explain it away, preserving the original interpretation instead of reconsidering it. We therefore introduce DEAR (Decoupled Evidence Arbitration and Reading ), a training-free agent for video question answering. DEAR retains a standard reader and delegates evidence-consistency checking to an independent subagent. If no inconsistency is re-ported, the reader receives the original video. Otherwise, a selector assesses the relevance and cred-ibility of the conflicting evidence without a fixed modality priority and chooses content to withhold. Blinding changes the media available to the reader, which responds in a fresh context without the conflict report, selection rationale, or earlier responses. This separates deciding which evidence to use from reasoning toward a response. Across three proprietary models on 500 source videos, DEAR raises accuracy under misleading claims from 47.2–64.0% to 81.8–85.2%, reduces trap-answer se-lection from 34.4–50.8% to 11.6–15.0%, and preserves no-claim accuracy. Our contributions are as follows: • We identify claim deference to rendered on-screen claims as a failure mode in video MLLMs and document claim anchoring : models cite the rendered claim as though it were visual evidence in reasoning traces. • We introduce VisualTrust , a controlled counterfactual benchmark that varies the rendered claim while holding the underlying video, question, and options fixed, isolating the effect of the claim on model responses. • We introduce DEAR , a training-free agent that separates evidence arbitration from reading via independent consistency checking, selective media blinding, and a fresh reader context. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 READING TEXT IN VIDEO 

Benchmarks such as Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025) and LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024) eval-uate video MLLMs across domains, durations, and question types. A complementary line targets text in video: BOVText (Wu et al., 2021) distinguishes titles, captions, and scene text, and MME-VideoOCR (Shi et al., 2025) evaluates OCR-based reasoning, including cross-frame integration. These benchmarks evaluate text recovery and text-based reasoning, rather than whether accepting an on-screen statement leads a model away from the visual evidence. 2.2 TEXT –V ISION CONFLICT AND CLAIM DEFERENCE 

When textual and visual signals disagree, VLMs place “blind faith in text”, disproportionately trust-ing the words (Deng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; 2025). Typographic attacks exploit this vul-nerability by planting scene-coherent text that misleads visual recognition and reasoning (Qraitem et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). These studies establish that introduced text can override visual evidence, primarily in single images. VisualTrust brings this question to video and measures how much an in-frame claim redirects a model’s response. Video also changes the structure of the arbitration, since a sampler may capture a transient claim while only partially capturing the distributed evidence needed to verify or refute it. Sycophancy work studies whether models abandon grounded responses to accommodate a mislead-ing user (Sharma et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). Recent video benchmarks apply the pressure through the conversational channel (Cao et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026); Cao et al. (2026) also use the term claim deference for prompt-level claims. VisualTrust instead places the claim inside the video. The model is never asked to endorse it, so the setup tests whether rendered language is treated as grounded visual evidence in the absence of conversational pressure. Hallu-cination benchmarks characterize an output-level property (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025); claim deference is one controlled input-side cause. 3The closest concurrent work is VisualTextTrap (Yakun et al., 2026), which studies text-overlay-induced hallucination using text-free, congruent, and contradictory conditions, and introduces a training-time mixture-of-experts mitigation. VisualTrust shares the controlled overlay comparison and the designated distractor, and adds matched single-point claim pairs and answer-source screen-ing, which isolate the effect of claim content. Its claim-fact classes span single-frame lookups through whole-video synthesis. DEAR mitigates at inference time, without retraining or access to model weights, and so applies to proprietary models. 2.3 CLAIM VERIFICATION AND MISINFORMATION 

Fact verification and misinformation detection predict a property of the content: whether a claim is supported, or an image–caption pair, thumbnail, or post is misleading (Thorne et al., 2018; Oguz et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2021; Naveed et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). VisualTrust intervenes on claim validity and measures the exposed model’s response. DEAR likewise answers the original question rather than delivering a verdict on a supplied claim. Verification also operates as an inference-time mechanism. Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2024) generates verification questions, answers them independently of the initial draft, and uses the checks to revise a response; VideoAgent (Wang et al., 2024) gathers question-relevant evidence through visual foundation-model tools. DEAR intervenes at a different point: its check runs with-out seeing any prior response, and its selection alters the media itself, controlling which evidence remains available during response generation. 

## 3 FORMULATING CLAIM DEFERENCE 

For questions about the recorded scene, a grounded model should follow visual evidence rather than a conflicting rendered claim. We call targeted reliance on such a claim claim deference .3.1 TASK SETUP

## 4 VISUAL TRUST : C ONTROLLED CLAIM INTERVENTIONS 

We instantiate this framework in VisualTrust . It comprises 5,063 matched three-condition cases over 2,811 unique source videos, drawn from five sources chosen to vary the provenance of the claim channel, the temporal spread of the evidence, and the density of ground-truth annotation. BOVText (Wu et al., 2021) supplies the claim channel in its native form, as original creator titles on short videos. Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025) adds long-form video, where a claim must be checked against evidence spread over minutes. Perception-Test (Patraucean et al., 2023), VidOR (Shang et al., 2019), and MME-VideoOCR (Shi et al., 2025) contribute short clips whose dense annotations cover actions and events, objects and their relations, and on-screen text, respectively. 4.1 CLAIM -B LINDED BASES 

Every video has one claim-blinded base. The no-claim condition is the base itself; the two claim conditions render their claim cards onto it. Whatever base preparation introduces is therefore com-mon to all three conditions. Where a clip carries an original creator claim, the base is produced by erasing that claim from its annotated region with the video inpainting model UnderEraser (Liu et al., 2026), and every repaired clip is manually reviewed. 54.2 CASE AND QUESTION CONSTRUCTION 

Each case is built in a fixed order (Figure 2). The question and options are specified first. We then establish the true value from visual evidence in the base and designate a conflicting value of the same fact as the trap answer. The accurate claim asserts the true value, and the misleading claim is derived from it by the single-point edit. The correct and trap answers are thus fixed before the paired claims are constructed. Questions are inherited where a source provides official QA (e.g., Video-MME) and newly authored where it does not (e.g., VidOR). All are multiple choice with three or four options. We retain only questions whose wording and video context rule out the rendered claim as a legitimate answer source; the test is a plain reading of the question, not the truth of the claim. Where necessary, we revise the question to specify the visual referent or source and re-screen it, without changing the options, correct answer, or trap answer. For example, “what is the price?” may license the overlay as a source, whereas “the price on the shelf tag” designates the in-scene one. The final question and options are held fixed across all three conditions. Cases with unresolved source ambiguity are excluded. No question mentions the claim card or instructs the model to disregard on-screen text. 4.3 CLAIM -F ACT CLASSES AND VALIDATION 

We assign each case one of 14 claim-fact labels according to the visually verifiable proposition changed by the misleading claim, ranging from locally checkable text and numeric readouts to whole-video propositions. The classes allow us to test whether claim deference extends across fact types (Section 5.2). Appendix B gives definitions, composition, and example pivots. For every released case, we verify that the pivoted fact holds in the video, that the paired claims differ only in that fact, and that the misleading claim reads as natural, genuinely conflicting on-screen text. Appendix A details question screening and claim validation. 4.4 RENDERED CLAIM CONDITIONS 

For each fixed claim pair, GPT-5.5 designs a claim card in the clip’s own visual language. The design specifies the full composition (layout, typography, color, backing, placement) and a single exposure episode aligned with the evidence that settles the claim. A renderer then draws the design onto the base. Both claim conditions share the same card design and exposure timing, differing only in the pivoted fact string. No released card occludes the pivot evidence or non-claim text needed to answer the question, so the correct answer remains recoverable from the visual evidence. Appendix C gives the full pipeline, including the render checks. 

## 5 MEASURING CLAIM DEFERENCE 

We ask three questions. Does a misleading claim redirect an otherwise correct response to the trap answer, and how broadly across fact types? Does higher no-claim accuracy imply greater resistance? And how do models justify the responses that flip? 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. We evaluate eleven video MLLMs spanning model families and parameter scales. Seven are open-weight: five Qwen3.5 variants from 9B to 397B (Qwen Team, 2026) and two Gemma 4 variants (Team et al., 2026). Four are proprietary: Qwen3.5-Plus (Qwen Team, 2026), Seed 2.1 Pro (ByteDance Seed, 2026), Gemini-3.5-Flash, and Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview (Google DeepMind, 2026b;a). Open-weight models are served via vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) with think-ing enabled; proprietary models are accessed through their official APIs at the highest available reasoning-effort setting for each model. For each case, every model answers the same question and options under all three claim conditions. Appendix D gives the remaining evaluation details. 5.2 MISLEADING CLAIMS REDIRECT CORRECT RESPONSES 

A misleading claim does not merely lower accuracy; it redirects responses toward the fact it asserts. Across the eleven models, mean accuracy falls from 90.1% with no claim to 51.9% with a misleading claim (Table 1). The diagnostic result is where the lost responses go: among responses correct with no claim, 42.8% flip to the trap answer under the misleading claim, only 0.6% move to any other error, and the rest remain correct (Figure 3b). Within every model, the trap answer accounts for 96.6– 99.4% of newly incorrect responses. The new errors are therefore overwhelmingly claim-directed rather than dispersed across the other options. This is claim deference. Accurate claims instead raise mean accuracy to 98.5% (Figure 3a). The accurate and misleading claims of a case are matched in presentation and exposure, differing only in the fact they assert. Their contrasting effects connect the response changes to claim content rather than to the presence of an overlay alone. Claims thus move responses in both directions: toward the truth when accurate, and toward the trap when misleading. Claim deference extends across all 14 claim-fact classes, from text and numeric readouts to actions, relations, and events. Response flips establish the direction of the error; reasoning traces reveal models’ stated justifica-tions. We analyze 600 traces from the main evaluation of Qwen3.5-Plus, Seed 2.1 Pro, and Gemini-3.5-Flash. For each model, we take cases answered correctly with no claim and sample 100 whose response flips to the trap answer under the misleading claim and 100 whose response stays correct. Across the three models, 75–100% of flip traces cite the rendered claim as evidence, against 4–5% of still-correct traces. We call this stated use of a claim as a premise claim anchoring . Appendix E gives the annotation protocol and per-model results. Anchoring persists even when a trace notes the contradiction. Among flip traces that acknowledge conflicting evidence, 86% explain the discrepancy away and continue to rely on the claim, reinterpreting object identities, temporal order, or the question’s intended source. In these traces, the conflict becomes something to reconcile with the claim rather than a reason to reconsider it. Appendix G reproduces two such traces in full. This pattern motivates DEAR’s separation of evidence arbitration from reading. 

## 6 MITIGATING CLAIM DEFERENCE 

To mitigate claim deference, we introduce DEAR (Decoupled Evidence Arbitration and Reading ), a training-free agent for video question answering. When DEAR detects conflicting accounts of a question-relevant fact, it selects evidence to withhold; otherwise, it leaves the video unchanged. An independent reader then answers using the resulting media and the original question and options, without receiving the conflict report or selection rationale. Detection, selection, and reading use the same video MLLM, rather than a stronger model. 6.1 DECOUPLED EVIDENCE ARBITRATION AND READING 

DEAR retains a standard video-question-answering reader that responds from the video, question, and options. In the reasoning traces we inspected, however, models often acknowledge inconsistent evidence but explain it away. Self-review can thus reinforce an interpretation rather than reconsider its basis. We measure final-response ac-curacy under each claim condition and trap-answer selection under misleading claims. Execution failures remain in the assigned denominator. DEAR makes two model calls per question without an intervention and three with one. Component ablations of DEAR are reported in Appendix F. DEAR raises accuracy under misleading claims to 81.8–85.2% across the three backbones, from 47.2–64.0% for the benchmark-prompt Direct and at most 70.0% for the strongest prompt-level baseline, and cuts trap-answer selection to 11.6–15.0% from 34.4–50.8% (Table 2). No-claim accuracy rises for every backbone. Accurate-claim accuracy is higher for Qwen3.5-Plus and Gemini-3.5-Flash, and unchanged for Seed 2.1 Pro. The reader-prompt Direct and Self-Review lower the trap-answer rate by at most 7.6 points relative to the benchmark-prompt Direct, compared with 21.6–36.0 points for DEAR. Reviewing a response in the same context leaves its interpretation 9in place, consistent with the explain-away pattern in the traces. Appendix G.2 follows one misleading-claim input through all four methods. Without blinding, Qwen3.5-Plus falls to 52.4% misleading-claim accuracy; arbitration history costs 8.8 points (Appendix F). No condition falls below the benchmark-prompt Direct. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

VisualTrust shows models can recognize a fact yet abandon it when an on-screen claim asserts otherwise, with errors concentrated on the asserted alternative. Resistance to such framing should therefore be evaluated separately from visual accuracy. Reasoning traces suggest why noticing a disagreement is insufficient. In the traces we inspected, a model that acknowledges the conflict often retains the claim as a premise and explains the conflict away. DEAR separates evidence arbitration from reading, changing what the reader can access without supplying an interpretation for it to adopt. On VisualTrust, it restores most of what misleading claims remove while preserving the direct answering path. The broader challenge is to preserve the value of on-screen language while grounding responses in evidence appropriate to the question.

## A CASE CONSTRUCTION AND SCREENING 

We use official questions and answers where available and author questions for videos without suit-able QA. Each question targets a visually verifiable fact and is paired with multiple-choice options. We screen questions for whether the rendered claim could be a legitimate answer source, regardless of whether the claim is true. If so, we rewrite the question to identify the intended object, event, or in-scene source while preserving the options and correct answer; cases that remain ambiguous are excluded. For example, a price question can specify the shelf tag as its source. Retained questions must exclude the rendered claim as an answer source through their wording or video context. Merely adding “in the video” does not resolve this ambiguity. Before writing the claims, we verify the correct answer against the video and designate the trap answer. The claim pairs are model-authored and manually reviewed to ensure that the accurate claim agrees with the visual evidence, the misleading claim contradicts it, and the two differ only in the targeted fact while remaining natural in the source language.

## F DEAR I MPLEMENTATION AND EVALUATION 

F.1 INFORMATION AVAILABLE TO EACH STAGE 

The independent consistency-checking subagent receives only the submitted video and original question stem. Its report identifies question-relevant disagreements, the accounts involved, and their sources and locations. A nonempty report triggers evidence selection; otherwise, the original video goes to the reader. Detecting a disagreement does not determine which account to trust. The selector receives the original video, question and options, and public report. It can retrieve OCR tracks by time and region, select text by track ID, or specify other visual content or the audio track for exclusion. The blind answer tool applies these selections to the media. PaddleOCR supplies text detections at a fixed rate of 2 frames per second. Optical flow tracks regions between detections, which are associated using text, appearance, and geometry. Changes in text content form separately selectable segments. Text selections mask all tracked spans, including those outside the query window. Each boundary adds a 0.5-second buffer, limited by the video bounds and known unselected text in the same region. F.2 MEDIA TRANSFORMATION AND READER ISOLATION 

Blinding preserves video duration and frame timing (Table 9). Masking re-encodes affected portions and copies the rest; encoding can also alter pixels outside the masks within those portions. Audio is retained unless removed, and audio-only removal preserves the video stream. The reader receives the resulting media, original question and options, and neutral indicators of unavailable content in a fresh context. It receives no conflict report, selection rationale, earlier responses, or OCR transcriptions. Its instructions distinguish unavailable evidence from evidence of absence. ## H LIMITATIONS 

VisualTrust isolates changes to a single fact while holding the video and question fixed. Real videos may contain several interacting claims, mix accurate and inaccurate statements, or leave the rele-vant events only partially visible. Extending the benchmark to these settings would test how models 24 weigh evidence when conflicts are less localized and the visual account is incomplete. Each model also uses its recommended video preprocessing, so cross-model differences reflect both model be-havior and the visual input each model receives. DEAR is evaluated on VisualTrust with three proprietary models and one question per source video. Its effectiveness on other benchmarks and open-weight models remains to be tested. The method depends on locating conflicting evidence and separating it from information needed to answer the question. A check may miss a disagreement, a selector may remove necessary evidence, or a text mask may leave part of a claim visible. When conflicting and necessary evidence occupy the same region or interval, blinding can remove both and leave the reader unable to answer. 25
