## Abstract 

Automated fact-checking (AFC) systems powered by large language model (LLM) agents still face challenges in evidence retrieval and claim verification. Existing approaches improve their capabilities through computationally costly post-training or human-in-the-loop feedback that is difficult to scale. To transfer professional fact-checking expertise to LLM agents efficiently, we distill publicly available guidelines published by Agence France-Presse (AFP) Fact Check into a reusable, modular Professional Fact-Checking Skill (PFC-Skill ). We then present SkillCheck , the first AFC sys-tem that gives LLM agents dynamic access to PFC-Skill to guide evidence retrieval and claim verification at inference time. Exten-sive experiments demonstrate that SkillCheck improves upon skill-free DEFAME baselines across multiple LLM backbones, with gains of 2.2–4.8 pp. in Accuracy and 1.5–6.3 pp. in Macro-F1 on the AVeriTeC benchmark. Notably, SkillCheck with GPT-4o-Mini exceeds DEFAME with GPT-4o by 3.4 pp. in Accuracy while cut-ting inference cost by roughly 61%. The performance gains also extend to open-web retrieval. Ablations show that incorporating the skill during evidence retrieval provides most of the gains, while incorporation into both stages yields the best overall performance. Case studies show that PFC-Skill enables more targeted search and closer scrutiny of evidence. To conclude, our work shows that LLM agents can “stand on the shoulders of giants” by drawing on the best practices of professional fact-checkers, substantially enhancing AFC performance without extra training or continuous human supervision.

However, these methods inevitably incur prohibitive computa-tional costs [8, 30, 37]. Alternatively, Sahnan et al. [34] propose a training-free method that incorporates a human-in-the-loop mecha-nism at inference time to provide feedback on AFC outputs. Yet this approach relies heavily on manual labor and thus struggles to scale to the massive volume of misinformation . Conse-quently, closing the gap between LLMs and human experts on AFC tasks hinges on a crucial question: How can we transfer human fact-checking expertise to LLM agents in a cost-efficient and scalable manner to enhance their AFC capabilities? 

Recent advances in agent skill use suggest a potent paradigm for transferring human expertise to LLM agents without repeatedly involving humans in the loop [13, 23, 26, 43]. This approach encodes human expertise into reusable guidance (i.e., skills) that agents can consult on demand. However, applying this paradigm to AFC remains underexplored, raising three key questions: (i) How can we identify the best practices of professional fact-checkers and distill them into agent skills? (ii) How can these skills be effectively incorporated into evidence retrieval and claim verification within AFC systems based on LLM agents? (iii) What impact do these skills have on the performance and inference cost of AFC systems? To bridge this gap, we draw on publicly available guidelines pub-lished by professional fact-checking organizations. In particular, we focus on the guidelines of Agence France-Presse (AFP) Fact Check. 2

These guidelines codify the standard operating procedures (SOPs) employed by AFP and detail key principles such as how to search for images, when and how to cross-check multiple independent sources, and how to verify quotes and assess factual claims. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we first distill these guidelines into a reusable, modular agent skill, termed the Professional Fact-Checking Skill (PFC-Skill ). Then, we introduce SkillCheck , the first AFC sys-tem that equips LLM agents with dynamic access to PFC-Skill at inference time, enabling more effective fact-checking without additional model training or human-in-the-loop feedback. 

Experiments show that SkillCheck improves upon the SOTA skill-free DEFAME baselines [9], achieving gains of 2.2–4.8 pp. in Accuracy and 1.5–6.3 pp. in Macro-F1. Notably, SkillCheck with GPT-4o-Mini surpasses DEFAME with GPT-4o by 3.4 pp. in Accu-racy, while reducing inference cost by approximately 61%. Open-web evaluation shows that these performance gains extend from con-trolled knowledge-base settings to open-web retrieval. Ablation studies indicate that skill incorporation during ER provides most performance gains, while incorporation into both ER and CV yields the best AFC performance. Case studies illustrate how skill guid-ance enables LLM agents to generate targeted queries for evidence retrieval and conduct closer scrutiny during claim verification. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• PFC-Skill. We introduce the first agent skill for fact-checking, distilling AFP’s expertise into reusable, modular guidance for evidence retrieval and claim verification. 

• SkillCheck. We present the first AFC system that gives LLM agents dynamic access to PFC-Skill at inference time.

## 2 Related Work 

Automated Fact-Checking (AFC). As discussed above, state-of-the-art AFC systems using LLM agents [9, 15, 33, 47] still lag behind human experts in both evidence retrieval and claim verification [12, 17, 18, 20]. To close this gap, recent studies explore post-training via supervised fine-tuning [17, 27, 39, 47] or reinforcement learning [19, 49, 52], but these methods incur prohibitive computational costs and contradict the principles of green AI [8, 30, 32, 37]. Alternatively, a concurrent study [34] proposes a training-free human-in-the-loop framework that leverages expert feedback at inference time, yet this approach relies heavily on manual labor [22]. In contrast, we introduce SkillCheck, a skill-based AFC system that transfers professional fact-checking know-how into a reusable, modular agent skill (PFC-Skill) and incorporates it into both stages without model training or human-in-the-loop feedback. Skills for LLM Agents. Skills are reusable, modular procedural instructions written in natural language that encode step-by-step expertise for specific tasks and can be dynamically invoked by LLM agents at inference time [4–6]. Prior work has used skills to improve tool use [10], planning [42], web navigation [43], and task orchestration [50]. There are broadly two routes to constructing skills: AI-crafted skills, which are synthesized by agents from their own successful trajectories [26, 42], and human-crafted skills, in which procedural knowledge is provided by human experts [4, 23, 45]. Empirical evaluations report limited or negative gains from AI-crafted skills in many tasks, while human-crafted skills can provide more reliable improvements [23]. These findings motivate the use of documented human expertise as the primary source of agent skills. However, in AFC, it remains unclear how to distill human expertise into agent skills, how to incorporate them effectively into evidence retrieval and claim verification, and how they affect LLM agents’ AFC performance. We investigate these questions by distilling AFP’s publicly available guidelines into PFC-Skill and evaluating its incorporation into both stages through SkillCheck. 

## 3 The SkillCheck System 

In this section, we introduce the SkillCheck system by detailing its two core components: skill creation , which builds the unified PFC-Skill from the AFP guidelines, and skill incorporation , which provides agents with targeted guidance during fact-checking.

Skill Organization. To make the distilled procedures accessible on demand, we structure PFC-Skill into two main components following prior work [4, 6], as illustrated in Fig. 3: high-level skill-usage instructions ( SKILL.md ) and a set of reference files. 3 Formally, we represent PFC-Skill as S = (𝐼, 𝑅 ), where 𝐼 denotes the instruc-tions in SKILL.md and 𝑅 denotes the collection of reference files. Each reference file 𝑟 𝑖 ∈ 𝑅 outlines the procedure for a particular fact-checking subtask, such as claim decomposition, source credibility assessment, or cross-verification. For example, the cross-verification reference file provides detailed steps for evalu-ating source independence, comparing evidence across sources, and resolving conflicting information. This organization enables agents to efficiently access procedural guidance during fact-checking.

## 4 Experiments 

In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate SkillCheck. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We use AVeriTeC [36] as our primary benchmark. It comprises English textual claims collected from 50 real-world fact-checking organizations and annotated with four veracity labels: Refuted, Supported, NEI, and Conflicting/Cherry-picking. 5

Unlike earlier datasets such as FEVER [38], AVeriTeC mitigates evidence leakage and insufficiency [35, 36], rendering it a more reliable benchmark for assessing automated fact-checking (AFC) systems under realistic conditions. We use the 500-claim develop-ment split of AVeriTeC. We remove duplicate claims based on claim text, resulting in 491 unique claims for evaluation. Each claim is accompanied by a knowledge base (KB) that contains both sup-porting evidence and a large number of distractor documents. This setup simulates open-web retrieval while ensuring reproducibility [35]. Following prior work [9, 17, 33, 47], for each query, we re-trieve the top five evidence items from the KB via semantic search with gte-base-en-v1.5 [51]. 6 To further assess whether the per-formance gains generalize to an open-web setting, we evaluate SkillCheck on ClaimReview2024+ [9], a collection of 300 Eng-lish claims drawn from the ClaimReview project. 7 Each claim is annotated with one of four veracity labels: Refuted, Supported, Misleading, or NEI. 8 These claims were drawn from fact-checking articles published from November 2023 onward, after GPT-4o’s Oc-tober 2023 knowledge cutoff, reducing the risk of knowledge con-tamination [9, 16]. To avoid temporal leakage in evidence, we retain only claims with explicit release dates, resulting in a filtered subset of 199 claims, denoted ClaimReview2024+ date . For each claim, we restrict evidence to documents published before the claim’s release date. We use date-range filters (e.g., before:YYYY-MM-DD ) during search. On this dataset, LLM agents interact with the open web via Google Web Search, Image Search, Reverse Image Search, and geolocation tools through Serper 9 and Google Vision. 10 

Baselines. We compare SkillCheck against two categories of baselines: post-training methods and training-free methods .

Post-training methods enhance LLM agents’ fact-checking performance via additional training. For example, HerO [47] ap-plies supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the AVeriTeC training split. Similarly, DebateCV [17] employs opposing debater agents and fine-tunes a moderator on synthetic debate transcripts to generate verdicts and justifications. Both methods rely on fine-tuned LLM agents; we report results for their Llama-3.1-8B implementations as our baselines. Results for both baselines are taken from [17]. 

Training-free methods perform fact-checking without any fine-tuning. Zero-shot directly prompts an LLM to predict a veracity label from the claim and evidence. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts the LLM to reason step by step before producing a verdict. Results

Ablations. To assess our design choices, we examine how PFC-Skill is created and where it is incorporated into AFC. For skill creation , we first compare SkillCheck with skill-free DEFAME (w/o Skill ) to assess the benefit of PFC-Skill. We then compare SkillCheck, which uses distilled PFC-Skill, with the w/o Distilla-tion variant, which provides raw AFP guidelines during evidence retrieval (ER) and claim verification (CV), to assess the added value of distillation. Since ER and CV involve distinct procedures, we also compare SkillCheck with stage-specific skills ( w/ Stage-Specific Skill ), which provide separate guidance for ER and CV. For skill incorporation , we isolate the effects of guidance at each stage by comparing three settings: skill-free DEFAME ( w/o ER & CV , equiva-lent to w/o Skill ), guidance during ER only ( w/o CV ), and guidance during both ER and CV (SkillCheck). 

LLM Backbones. Our experiments cover closed-source and 

open-weight LLM backbones. The closed-source models are GPT-4o-2024-08-06 (hereafter GPT-4o ) and GPT-4o-Mini-2024-07-18 (GPT-4o-Mini ). We also evaluate the open-weight DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 (hereafter DeepSeek-V4-Flash ) and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B . We use a temperature of 0.01 to reduce sampling variability. 

Metrics. We use two metrics: (1) Accuracy (Acc.) , defined as the proportion of correctly classified claims; and (2) Macro-F1 , the unweighted mean F1 score across all classes. Results are averaged over at least three repeated runs. For statistical rigor, we report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) for the mean scores, computed using 10,000 resamples of matched claims.

## 4.4 Ablation Studies 

To explain the performance gains, we compare the ablation variants defined in Sec. 4.1 on AVeriTeC using GPT-4o and GPT-4o-Mini. 

Skill Creation. As shown in Fig. 6(a), PFC-Skill is more ef-fective than directly using the raw AFP guidelines. The w/o Distillation variant improves Accuracy over w/o Skill by only 0.4 and 1.2 pp. for GPT-4o and GPT-4o-Mini, respectively. PFC-Skill adds a further 3.6 pp. in Accuracy for both backbones and 4.0 and 3.9 pp. in Macro-F1, respectively. These results suggest that access to profes-sional knowledge alone provides limited benefits. This may be because the raw guidelines combine broad principles with procedures for human fact-checkers. Distilling them into PFC-Skill makes the relevant knowledge actionable for LLM agents by specifying pro-cedures for evidence retrieval and claim verification. Among the distilled variants, w/ Stage-Specific Skill provides no consistent advantage over SkillCheck with the unified PFC-Skill . For GPT-4o, w/ Stage-Specific Skill reduces both Accuracy and Macro-F1 by 0.8 pp. compared with SkillCheck. For GPT-4o-Mini, it reduces Accuracy by 0.8 pp. but increases Macro-F1 by 1.1 pp. We there-fore use a single skill package for both stages, avoiding the need to construct and maintain separate stage-specific skills. 

Skill Incorporation. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the w/o CV variant accounts for most of the performance gains, while extending skill use to CV provides additional improvements . The w/o CV variant improves Accuracy and Macro-F1 by 3.0 and 4.5 pp. for GPT-4o, and by 3.0 and 3.1 pp. for GPT-4o-Mini. These gains account for approximately 55–75% of the improvements achieved by SkillCheck. Adding CV guidance in SkillCheck further improves Accuracy and Macro-F1 by 1.0 and 1.8 pp. for GPT-4o, and by 1.8 and 2.5 pp. for GPT-4o-Mini. These results indicate that our PFC-Skill offers complementary benefits at different stages, motivating skill incorporation at both fact-checking stages.

## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce PFC-Skill , an agent skill distilled from AFP Fact Check’s professional guidelines, and propose SkillCheck, a training-free AFC system that dynamically selects relevant skills to guide evidence retrieval and claim verification. Experiments demonstrate that SkillCheck outperforms the previous state-of-the-art skill-free AFC system, DEFAME. Furthermore, SkillCheck with GPT-4o-Mini achieves higher AFC performance at lower infer-ence cost than skill-free DEFAME with GPT-4o. Variation in gains across LLM backbones also motivates future research in skill learn-ing to enhance LLMs’ ability to apply skills. Ablation studies show that evidence retrieval guidance accounts for most gains, while claim verification guidance provides further improvements. Case studies illustrate that skill guidance enables more targeted evidence retrieval and closer scrutiny during claim verification. ## C Definitions of the Veracity Labels C.1 AVeriTeC 

AVeriTeC defines four veracity labels [36]. We use shortened names below. The label Not Enough Evidence is denoted by NEI: 

• Supported : the claim is supported by the available evidence. 

• Refuted : the claim is contradicted by the available evidence. 

• NEI (Not Enough Evidence ): the available evidence is insufficient to verify or refute the claim. 

• Conflicting/Cherry-picking : the claim is misleading due to conflicting evidence or omission of important context. 

## C.2 ClaimReview2024+ date 

ClaimReview2024+ date uses four veracity labels defined in [9]: 

• Supported : the claim is accurate based on the available evi-dence. 

• Refuted : the available evidence contradicts the claim. 

• Misleading : the claim is misleading or requires additional context. 

• NEI (Not Enough Information ): there is insufficient evidence to verify the claim.
