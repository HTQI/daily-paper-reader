A B S T R A C T 

Vision–language pre-training has enabled substantial progress in image–text retrieval and zero-shot recognition by learning transferable cross-modal representations. However, sentence-level alignment remains insufficient for compositional matching, where captions can describe the same objects and attributes while differing in how these elements are bound. To address this limitation, we propose Flow-CLIP, which refines global image–text alignment through image-conditioned changes in token– patch evidence. Global Compatibility Calibration (GCC) learns a sentence-level similarity metric, while Image-Conditioned Evidence Refinement (ICER) incorporates visual patch context into token queries and uses the resulting change in local matching evidence to adjust the global score. We further introduce Cross-Image Margin Ranking (CIMR), which compares the same positive–negative caption pair across matched and mismatched images, encouraging a larger ranking margin when the image supports the correct description. Experiments on compositional matching and attribute–object binding benchmarks show that Flow-CLIP improves binding accuracy over pretrained CLIP and learned global alignment through local evidence refinement, while cross-image ranking supervision mitigates reliance on language priors. 1. Introduction 

Vision–language pretraining enables zero-shot recog-nition and image–text retrieval by learning transferable representations from paired images and descriptions [1–5]. Compositional matching requires these representations to distinguish descriptions that name the same objects and attributes but assign or relate them differently. Benchmarks of attribute binding, relations and word order document persistent errors in making these distinctions [6–11]. The caption rankings in Fig. 1 illustrate how CLIP-based models [1, 7, 12] can prefer a hard-negative caption to the positive caption. The alternatives share many words but differ in object identities, attribute assignments or spatial relations.

Targeted caption negatives expose these distinctions during training [7, 13], while dense descriptions and image–text– graph comparisons add supervision for scene details [14, 15]. FSC-CLIP [12] combines local hard-negative training with calibrated regularization to improve compositional matching while maintaining general-task performance. LABCLIP [16] shows that a learned linear transformation of text embeddings can recover attribute–object binding information in cross-modal matching. We use learned global alignment as a baseline to measure the additional contribution of image-conditioned local refinement to attribute–object binding. Local matching provides the representations needed for such refinement. FILIP [17] and DCSM [18] use token–patch similarities, ComCLIP [19] and OC-CLIP [20] introduce structured entity scores, and FLAIR [21] constructs caption-conditioned visual representations. These methods aggregate local similarities, compare structured entities or condition visual features on text. We use local interactions to measure how image context changes support for a caption, providing a correction to its calibrated global score. An incorrect attribute assignment can receive local support when its individual objects and attributes are present in the image. Strong token–patch matches alone therefore leave open whether the proposed assignment is supported. We use the image’s patch context to refine the token queries that assess this assignment, then compare the refined and original queries on the same patches. The original query supplies a reference for measuring the correction’s effect, yielding a signed adjustment that can strengthen or weaken the global compatibility score. To model this image-conditioned change, we propose Flow-CLIP, which combines Global Com-patibility Calibration (GCC) with Image-Conditioned Evidence Refinement (ICER) , as illustrated in Fig. 2. Full-caption hard negatives supervise caption ranking on matched images, following NegCLIP [7]. The construction of these alternatives also matters: SugarCrepe [22] iden-tifies plausibility and fluency biases that allow models to distinguish positive descriptions from negative alternatives without the image. Language priors can consequently favor plausible descriptions even when their attribute assignments are unsupported by the visual input. Supervision on matched pairs does not explicitly constrain how this preference should change across images. A caption preference that persists after replacing the image can satisfy the matched-pair objective without reflecting the visual evidence for the attribute assign-ment. We introduce Cross-Image Margin Ranking (CIMR) 

to compare the same positive–negative caption margin on the matched image and one donor shared by both captions. By encouraging a larger margin on the matched image, this objective rewards a change in preference tied to visual input while holding the captions and their linguistic cues fixed. GCC and ICER are optimized jointly with the hard-negative and CIMR objectives. Independently trained global-only heads account for much of the caption-order improvement, while training with the local branch yields additional attribute–object binding gains. Paired predictions identify corrected binding errors and new spatial errors. Image-replacement tests measure changes in caption preference when the visual match is removed. Comparisons across training sources and encoders show how binding gains vary alongside changes in spatial and retrieval accuracy. The contributions of this work are: • We propose Flow-CLIP, combining Global Compati-bility Calibration (GCC) and Image-Conditioned Evi-dence Refinement (ICER) with frozen encoders. The local branch refines token queries using visual patch context and adjusts global compatibility by the signed change in pooled patch evidence. • We introduce Cross-Image Margin Ranking (CIMR), which compares the same positive–negative caption margin on matched and shared donor images. This objective encourages caption preference to depend on the matched image using full-caption supervision. • Our experiments separate the binding gains of local refinement from the sentence-level gains of global calibration and identify the contribution of cross-image supervision to mitigating reliance on language priors. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Compositional Evaluation 

Compositional benchmarks evaluate image–text match-ing through controlled semantic changes. Winoground [6] pairs two images with captions that share their words but differ in composition, requiring scores to recover both image– caption assignments. ARO [7] separates attribution, relation and caption-order judgments. VL-CheckList [8] organizes object, attribute and relation edits, while VALSE [9] evaluates linguistic phenomena using positive descriptions and edited foils. What’s Up? [11] focuses on spatial relations. These distinctions matter for local scoring: recognizing the concepts 

2present in an image can support object discrimination, whereas attribute reassignment and relation reversal require resolving how those concepts are associated. CLEVR [23] and PUG [24] provide rendered scenes with known attribute–object assignments. Swapping those assign-ments produces caption alternatives that preserve the scene’s object and attribute inventory, isolating the assignment distinction relevant to token-level refinement. CREPE [25] tests systematicity and productivity with candidate sets of increasing compositional complexity, and BiVLC [26] evaluates both retrieval directions. These evaluations extend the comparison to competition among multiple descriptions and to the consistency of image–text matching across retrieval directions. Caption construction determines which cues can support a correct ranking. SugarCrepe [22] uses fluent semantic edits to reduce linguistic artifacts, and SugarCrepe++ [27] distinguishes semantic sensitivity from lexical variation. NegBench [28] evaluates negation, whereas Half-Truths [29] adds plausible but incorrect content to an otherwise valid description. Controlling the caption alternatives limits cues introduced by their construction. Image-replacement controls address the complementary question of whether the resulting preference depends on the visual match while keeping those alternatives fixed. 

2.2. Image–Text Matching 

Global scoring compares pooled image and text embed-dings, as used in CLIP [1] and ALIGN [2]. LiT [4] studies alignment with a locked image encoder. LABCLIP [16] evaluates binding within each modality using linear probes, increasing object counts and conjunctive search, then learns a text-embedding transformation for cross-modal matching. These experiments separate the information represented by each encoder from the information accessible through the cross-modal scoring function. Work on concept-to-object mappings [30] further ex-amines how embeddings represent binding functions. The distinction relevant to our comparison is the granularity of the learned alignment: LABCLIP transforms the pooled text em-bedding, while ICER updates individual token queries using image patches. GCC retains the sentence-level transformation, allowing the experiments to assess the additional effect of training with the local branch under matched conditions. Fine-grained alignment preserves visual and textual units within the matching score. RegionCLIP [31] learns region–text representations, and FILIP [17] aggregates token-wise maximum similarities for cross-modal late interaction. FLAIR [21] uses text-conditioned attention pooling over image tokens for detailed retrieval, while Beta-CLIP [32] develops text-conditioned alignment at multiple granularities. The conditioning differs across these methods: late interaction selects matching responses, whereas text-conditioned pooling changes the visual representation. DCSM [18] learns a scoring function over dense token– patch cosine similarities, including functional rows for spatial relationship words. ICER scores the change in pooled support after query correction. Structured matching specifies the semantic units used for comparison. ComCLIP [19] computes separate scores for subject, object and action subimages, then combines these scores at inference. OC-CLIP [20] associates scene-graph nodes with visual slots and scores relational compat-ibility, while ABE-CLIP [33] introduces attribute-binding operations during inference. VQAScore [34] scores image– text compatibility through visual question answering. Flow-CLIP uses contextual token and patch states as its local comparison units, without requiring explicit scene-graph or subimage decomposition. Full-caption supervision trains the contribution of these interactions to the final compatibility score. 

2.3. Training Objectives 

Compositional training exposes distinctions that ordi-nary image–caption pairs may leave implicit. NegCLIP [7] uses caption perturbations and hard image negatives, and TSVLC [13] manipulates language structure to teach at-tributes, relations and states from paired data. DAC [14] supplies dense, image-aligned descriptions. Coarse-to-fine image–text–graph learning [15] decomposes and augments parsed scene graphs to align descriptions of different struc-tural complexities with an image. TripletCLIP [35] and SPARCL [36] add synthetic visual and textual comparisons. Flow-CLIP trains its global and local terms through the same image–caption score using full-caption hard negatives. The objective determines which relationships those train-ing examples constrain. CE-CLIP [37] introduces intra-modal contrast and cross-modal hard-negative ranking. FSC-CLIP [12] combines local hard-negative loss with selective calibrated regularization to balance compositional supervi-sion and general multimodal capabilities. Its design addresses the degradation of pretrained multimodal representations caused by hard-negative fine-tuning. READ-CLIP [38] cou-ples token reconstruction with paraphrase alignment, while concept-centric learning [39] aligns caption concepts with visual content through auxiliary contrasts. These objectives constrain feature alignment or its preservation during adapta-tion. CLIC [40] constructs training comparisons from com-binations of multiple images and their captions. CIMR evaluates the same caption pair on both the matched image and a shared donor image. Caption identity remains fixed when the two margins are compared, so an additive text-only term cancels and the objective constrains the score change induced by replacing the image.

4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup Training data. We train on COCO [41] train2014 with the Karpathy restval split [42], using 113,287 training im-ages, 566,435 image–caption records and 25,000 valida-tion records. Each training record pairs a human-written COCO caption [43] with a full-caption hard negative. For the training-source comparison, we use Conceptual Cap-tions (CC3M) [44] with the CC3M-100K protocol of FSC-CLIP [12]: 101,830 source records partitioned into 97,734 training and 4,096 validation records. CoCa [45] supplies positive captions, and the negative-generation schedule uses phrase swaps, WordNet replacements and bigram shuffling. The released training and validation partitions share nine images, represented by 138 training and 16 validation records. 

Evaluation datasets and metrics. We evaluate attri-bution, relations and caption order separately. ARO [7] contains four subsets: VG-Attribution (4,517 images and 28,748 pairs), VG-Relation (5,316 images and 23,937 pairs), Flickr30k Order (1,000 images and 5,000 five-caption sets), and COCO Order (5,000 images and 25,010 five-caption sets). SugarCrepe [22] contains 7,511 fluent positive–negative pairs with Add, Replace and Swap edits. CREPE-Productivity [25] evaluates Atomic and Swap candidate sets at complexity levels 4–12. Attribute–object binding evaluation uses ABC-6K [46] (3,213 comparison pairs), PUG:SPAR [24] (19,840 attribute-assignment pairs), and a two-object evaluation set derived from CLEVR [23]. We abbreviate ABC-6K and PUG:SPAR as ABC and PUG in the result tables. The CLEVR combination-based partition contains 470 records, including 449 valid pairs with non-equivalent captions; the larger CLEVR evaluation pool contains 4,449 unambiguous pairs. The ablation table reports the 449-pair split, and results on the 4,449-pair pool are identified separately in the text. COCO-Spatial and GQA-Spatial contain 2,687 and 1,451 records, respectively, and follow the spatial-relation protocol of What’s Up? [11]. Retrieval evaluation uses Flickr30k [47] and COCO [41] with the Karpathy 1K and 5K splits [42], respectively: 1,000/5,000 images and 5,000/25,000 captions. Zero-shot 

> 5

classification uses CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 [48] (10,000 test images each) and ImageNetV2 [49] (10,000 images with 1,000 class prompts). We retain each benchmark’s task-level aggregation and specify the members of every cross-task mean in its caption. Pairwise accuracy tests the gold caption against one foil; candidate-set accuracy requires it to rank first among all alternatives. Retrieval extends this competition to the complete evaluation gallery, testing whether the learned score separates the correct match from many alternatives beyond the constructed foils. Image and comparison counts are reported separately because an image can appear in several evaluation records. 

Implementation details. The main configuration freezes CLIP ViT-B/32 [1] and trains 𝑀 , 𝑀 tok and 𝑊 , totaling 786,432 parameters. For both head-only and encoder-adapted training, we use AdamW [50] with learning rate 8 × 10 −5 ,weight decay 10 −4 , (𝛽 1, 𝛽 2) = (0 .9, 0.999) and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1. Each batch contains 32 image–caption records and 32 full-caption negatives. COCO training uses five full epochs with a hard-negative pool of 501,921 eligible records. CC3M training uses three epochs, with 96,463, 97,394 and 97,734 valid records for phrase swaps, WordNet replacements and bigram shuffling, respectively. We set 𝜏 𝑐 = 0 .01 , 𝛼 = 1 and 𝜆 CIMR = 0 .1, with 

𝜏 𝑝 = 0 .05 for COCO and 0.06 for CC3M. Experiments use NVIDIA RTX 3090 and A800 GPUs. In the encoder-adapted variants, both the image and text encoders are trainable together with 𝑀 , 𝑀 tok and 𝑊 under the HNB and CIMR objectives. Their COCO and CC3M results are evaluated after five and three training epochs, respectively. The scoring equations and objectives remain the same in both variants, allowing the comparison to assess the effect of updating encoder representations alongside the scoring maps. 

Feature extraction. Scoring uses FP32 with autocast and TF32 disabled; normalized global and patch features are first rounded to FP16. CLIP [1] inputs follow the OpenAI image preprocessing pipeline. Patch states pass through vision post-layer normalization and visual projection. Text token states use the text projection and the valid-token mask defined in the method. SigLIP [5] retains its learned image-pooling operation and 196 patch embeddings, with a maximum text sequence length of 64 tokens in our evaluation. BLIP [3] uses 256-dimensional image–text contrastive (ITC) projections and 576 patch embeddings, with a maximum text sequence length of 35 tokens.

4.3. Ablation Studies 

We independently train Global-𝑀 and the global/local scorers with the same data and optimization settings, eval-uating all heads at COCO epoch 5. Means and standard deviations across three runs quantify training variability. 

Component effects. Global calibration accounts for much of the improvement over pretrained CLIP [1] in Tab. 2. With matched COCO data and optimization at epoch 5, Global-

𝑀 raises the 11-task mean from 55.95% to 67.73%. Its largest gains occur on caption order, with accuracy above 90% on both Flickr30k Order and COCO Order. Flow-CLIP reaches 71.10%, adding 3.37 percentage points to the mean. The binding tasks distinguish this additional benefit from the caption-order gains already obtained through global calibration. The seven-task binding mean covers Swap-A, Swap-O, ABC, COCO-Spatial, GQA-Spatial, PUG and the CLEVR 449-pair split. It rises from 54.63% for CLIP to 58.34% for Global-𝑀 and 62.64% for Flow-CLIP: global calibration adds 3.70 percentage points, and the joint scorer adds another 4.30 points. This group tests attribute assignments, object identity and spatial relations. The 11-task mean additionally includes all four ARO tasks, where global calibration already achieves high caption-order accuracy. For a fixed global mapping, the local contribution in Eq. (7) changes the caption-pair margin by 𝛼 [𝑠 token (𝐼, 𝑇 +) − 

𝑠 token (𝐼, 𝑇 −)] . Ranking therefore depends on the difference between the two evidence changes. Equal increments leave the margin unchanged; unequal increments can increase or decrease it. In the trained ablations, each scorer learns its own global mapping, jointly with the local maps where present. The HNB and CIMR comparisons measure the effect of training the global/local architecture under each objective. Controlled assignment tasks preserve the objects and attributes mentioned in the captions while changing which attribute belongs to each object. Under HNB, training the local branch with the global map raises accuracy on the CLEVR [23] 449-pair split from 44.99% to 53.67% and on PUG:SPAR [24] from 50.63% to 54.20%. With CIMR in both heads, Flow-CLIP exceeds Global-𝑀 +CIMR by 9.80 and 4.20 percentage points, respectively. Training with the local branch improves both assignment tasks under either objective. On the CLEVR 4,449-pair evaluation pool, HNB-

7trained Global-𝑀 achieves 51.29%, while Flow-CLIP trained with HNB and CIMR achieves 56.06%. Flow-CLIP exceeds Global-𝑀 by 5.31 percentage points on Swap-Object and 7.22 points on ABC, while COCO-Spatial [11] decreases by 1.38 points. The local architecture’s gains on binding thus differ from its effect on spatial-relation discrimination. Paired predictions distinguish corrected se-lections from new errors. 

Encoder adaptation. The encoder-update ablation com-pares the fully trainable Flow-CLIP models in Tab. 1 with sep-arate frozen Flow-CLIP runs on the corresponding training source. The frozen controls achieve 85.91% on COCO and 83.85% on CC3M, giving adaptation gains of 0.52 and 3.11 points, respectively. Within the matched COCO comparison, Flickr30k Order, COCO Order, Swap-Object and COCO-Spatial decrease by 1.20, 0.91, 1.63 and 0.49 points. The ARO increase comes from attribution and relation gains despite lower caption-order accuracy. 

Training variability. Across three independently trained COCO runs, mean ARO accuracy and its sample standard deviation are 84 .31 ± 0 .19 for Global-𝑀 , 84 .47 ± 0 .21 for Global-𝑀 +CIMR, 85 .77 ± 0 .25 for Flow-CLIP (HNB) and 

85 .90 ± 0 .04 for Flow-CLIP with CIMR. Flow-CLIP exceeds Global-𝑀 in every run. 

Confidence intervals. We estimate 95% percentile con-fidence intervals for three-run mean accuracy with 10,000 paired image-cluster bootstrap draws at fixed final check-points. Each draw uses the same image resampling weights across methods, tasks and training runs. The intervals for the Flow-CLIP minus Global-𝑀 accuracy difference are [1.26, 2.31] percentage points on SugarCrepe, [5.95, 8.13] on ABC and [ −3 .20 , −0 .04 ] on COCO-Spatial. They support a gain on SugarCrepe and ABC and a decline on COCO-Spatial under this evaluation protocol. Standard deviations across runs measure training vari-ability, while the paired bootstrap estimates image-sampling uncertainty in the three-run mean at fixed checkpoints. Each bootstrap draw must retain all benchmark aggregation groups. Some VG-Relation groups contain only one unique image, so we omit intervals for VG-Relation and the ARO mean under this protocol. We also omit GQA and image-replacement intervals because image-cluster membership, including shared donor groups, cannot be fully determined from the evaluation records.

Fixed uses one anchor image for all records within a task, removing this variation as well. The two replacement conditions test whether preference for the original gold caption persists across different replacement images or under one constant visual input.

Adding CIMR to Global-𝑀 also lowers ARO accuracy under image replacement, with little change in matched-image accuracy. For both architectures, caption-order tasks show larger reductions under Shuffle and Fixed than attribution or relation tasks. The caption candidates and their linguistic cues remain unchanged across these conditions. CIMR thus mitigates reliance on language priors in compositional matching, reducing the gold-caption preference retained under image replacement while maintaining matched-image accuracy. For Flow-CLIP with the CIMR objective in Eq. (11), image replacement produces a larger accuracy drop on ARO than on the six-task average over SugarCrepe, ABC and spatial-relation tasks, which remains 50.38% under Shuffle. The matched-image ablation in Tab. 2 shows that adding CIMR to the HNB-trained global/local scorer raises CLEVR and PUG accuracy by 2.45 and 0.85 percentage points, while Swap-Attribute, ABC, COCO-Spatial and GQA-Spatial decrease by 0.75, 0.22, 0.45 and 0.21 points. Lower caption-preference retention under image replace-ment coexists with gains and losses across the matched-image binding tasks.

5. Conclusion 

We presented Flow-CLIP to improve attribute–object binding and mitigate reliance on language priors in com-positional image–text matching. GCC calibrates global align-ment, ICER adjusts this score through image-conditioned changes in token–patch evidence, and CIMR supervises caption-pair margins across matched and replacement im-ages. Experiments show improved binding beyond learned global alignment and reduced reliance on language priors. These findings support using image-conditioned evidence refinement to resolve binding ambiguities that persist after sentence-level alignment.
