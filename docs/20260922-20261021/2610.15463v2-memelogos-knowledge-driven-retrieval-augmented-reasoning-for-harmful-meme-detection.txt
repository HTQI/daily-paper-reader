# MemeLogos: Knowledge-Driven Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning for Harmful Meme Detection 

Hexiang Gu, Saihui Hou, Member, IEEE , Changlan Liu, Min Ren, Huijia Wu, Hongxun Gu, and Zhaofeng He, Member, IEEE 

Abstract —With the rapid proliferation of social media, harmful memes have become a growing threat to online ecosystems. Their harmfulness is often implicitly conveyed through complex interactions among visual, textual, and contextual cues, making accurate detection challenging. Recent studies have introduced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and external knowl-edge into harmful meme detection. However, existing methods mainly retrieve similar instances or auxiliary information, lim-iting their ability to capture reusable harmfulness knowledge and underlying reasoning mechanisms, and thus constraining both detection performance and interpretability. To address these challenges, we propose MemeLogos, a knowledge-driven retrieval-augmented reasoning framework for harmful meme detection. MemeLogos abstracts recurring harmfulness patterns and reasoning structures from diverse harmful cases into reusable knowledge units and organizes them into a dedicated star-shaped knowledge graph. By combining structured knowledge retrieval with an explicit multi-stage reasoning process inspired by cognitive mechanisms, MemeLogos leverages retrieved knowl-edge to guide harmfulness reasoning, improving both detection reliability and interpretability. Extensive experiments show that MemeLogos consistently outperforms existing harmful meme detection methods while generating more coherent and inter-pretable reasoning, demonstrating the effectiveness of structured harmfulness knowledge for multimodal content understanding. 

Index Terms —Harmful meme detection, Knowledge graph, Retrieval-Augmented Generation

A. Harmful Meme Detection 

Harmful meme detection has become an important research topic for online content safety. Benchmark datasets such as Hateful Memes [1], HarMeme [9], MAMI [10], and Tox-iCN MM [11] have established diverse evaluation settings covering different harmfulness categories, languages, and so-cial contexts. Early approaches primarily focused on multi-modal feature extraction and fusion, while subsequent dual-stream architectures and vision-language pre-training mod-els [12], [13] enabled more effective cross-modal represen-tation learning. For instance, Hate-CLIPper [14] explicitly models interactions between CLIP-based visual and textual features to capture their complementary information for harm-fulness classification. Nevertheless, harmful meanings are frequently conveyed through implicit cross-modal interactions involving irony, metaphor, cultural references, and contextual incongruity, making them difficult to recognize through surface-level cor-relations alone. To address these challenges, recent studies have incorporated auxiliary semantic understanding and rea-soning into harmful meme detection. CapAlign [15] employs question-driven informative caption generation to enrich vi-sual semantics and improve cross-modal alignment. B. Graph-Based Retrieval-Augmented Generation 

Recent studies extend conventional Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) by explicitly modeling structured relations among external knowledge units. GraphRAG [19] constructs entity-centric graph indexes and community summaries to support global reasoning over large document collections, while G-Retriever [20] formulates retrieval over textual graphs as a subgraph selection problem. HippoRAG [21] combines knowledge graphs with graph-based propagation to support multi-hop knowledge integration, and KG 2RAG [22] ex-ploits knowledge-graph relations to expand and organize se-mantically retrieved evidence. Beyond retrieval, Think-on-Graph [23] and Reasoning on Graphs [24] explore explicit graph traversal and relation-path reasoning over knowledge graphs, while Think-on-Graph 2.0 [25] further integrates structured graph retrieval with unstructured contextual evi-dence. StructRAG [26] additionally demonstrates the benefit of dynamically organizing retrieved knowledge into task-appropriate structured representations. These studies highlight the importance of relational structure for knowledge retrieval and reasoning. Different from general Graph-RAG methods that mainly operate over entity-, document-, or fact-level structures, MemeLogos abstracts multimodal harmful cases into reusable rule-level knowledge and explicitly models se-mantic and polarity-aware relations among harmfulness rules, enabling task-specific retrieval and contrastive reasoning for harmful meme detection. A. Harmfulness Knowledge 

Existing methods often represent knowledge at the level of instances or unstructured textual explanations, making it difficult to capture inherent harmfulness patterns. Moreover, directly relying on models to generate knowledge from memes may introduce inherent semantic biases. To mitigate these issues, we ground harmfulness knowledge construction in meme instances and their detailed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) annotations. Specifically, leveraging the training data with detailed CoT annotations provided by MemeMind [27], we construct a harmfulness knowledge graph in two steps: 1) Formalized Rule Extraction, abstracting essential harmfulness patterns and rules from the memes with CoT annotations, and 2) Topological Graph Modeling, explicitly modeling harmful-ness rules via star-shaped topology. 

1) Formalized Rule Extraction: In this section, we leverage instance-level logic distillation and corpus-level rule general-ization to map unstructured multimodal memes to formalized harmfulness rule units, as illustrated in Figure 2, so as to accurately extract and generalize the inherent harmful logic hidden within heterogeneous memes. Instance-level logic distillation. Given a sample, we de-compose the instance into six logical primitives: 

zi = ( ai, m i, r i, t i, c i, v i), (1) where ai, mi, ri, ti, ci and vi respectively denote the anchor, capturing the observable visual or textual carrier of the meme; semantic mapping, represents the latent semantic interpretation assigned to the carrier; rhetorical mechanism, describing the compositional mechanism that connects the observable cue to the latent meaning; target, identifing the individual, group, institution, or social norm affected by the meme; consequence, recording the semantic or social effect and verdict, denoting the final harmfulness label. This logical decomposition reduces instance-specific multimodal noise while retaining the reason-ing structure required for harmfulness assessment.

Harmfulness rule units. Although multimodal memes vary considerably in content and presentation, recurring harm-fulness patterns often share a stable logical structure. We therefore canonicalize each retained cluster into a formalized rule unit:

2) Topological Graph Modeling: Harmfulness rule units extracted in the Formalized Rule Extraction stage are in-herently high-order relational objects composed of multiple logical primitives. Conventional entity-relation graphs only encode binary facts as independent triples, which cannot natively match the structure of rule units. Such representation fragments the internal semantics of rules. Therefore, to fully retain the semantic integrity of each rule, realize explicit structured modeling, and facilitate retrieval and evidence trac-ing, we reconstruct plain-text harmfulness rule units into a structured harmfulness knowledge graph embedded with star-shaped topology, as shown in Harmfulness Knowledge Graph of Figure 1, which contains star-shaped rule subgraphs and cross-rule relations. Star-shaped rule subgraphs. For each harmfulness rule unit, we construct a rule-centered local star subgraph to explicitly organize its semantic attributes, supporting evidence, and associated verdict. The central Rule node represents a generalized harmfulness pattern and is connected to six types of peripheral nodes: Anchor (Anch.), Rhetoric (Rhet.), Target (Targ.), Consequence (Cons.), Support cases (Case), and Verdict (Verd.). The first four capture the key semantic characteristics underlying the rule, while Case nodes preserve supporting instances as empirical evidence and the Verd. node specifies the corresponding harmfulness judgment. The Mapping primitive is not explicitly included in the graph, as it mainly captures instance-specific cross-modal corre-spondences rather than reusable rule-level attributes. This rule-centered organization separates generalized harmfulness knowledge from its supporting instances while preserving their explicit associations. We further define three types of structural relations to organize these dependencies. T RIGGERS edges (Trig.) connect Anch., Rhet., Targ., and Cons. nodes to the Rule node, representing semantic conditions relevant to rule applicabil-ity. S UPPORTS edges (Supp.) connect Case nodes to their corresponding Rule node, linking generalized knowledge to its supporting instances. C ONCLUDE edges (Concl.) connect each Rule node to its Verd. node, associating the rule with its harmfulness judgment. Together, these relations establish explicit topological paths, providing a structured foundation for rule matching, evidence tracing, and knowledge retrieval. 

Cross-rule relations. Although rule-centered star subgraphs explicitly organize the semantic attributes and supporting evidence of individual rules, they lack direct associations across different rule units, limiting the exploration of related knowledge beyond local structures. To address this limita-tion, we introduce two types of cross-rule semantic rela-tions based on semantic similarity and verdict polarity: (1) SEMANTICALLY ALIGNED edges (Align.) connect attribute nodes of the same semantic type when their similarity exceeds a threshold τa. These relations capture semantic associa-tions between attributes belonging to different rules, allowing retrieval to expand beyond directly matched attributes and identify relevant rules across different meme scenarios. This facilitates knowledge sharing and improves the accessibility of semantically related rule-level knowledge. (2) G UARDS 

AGAINST edges (Guard.) connect Rule nodes that exhibit high semantic similarity above a threshold τg but have op-posite verdicts. These relations capture potentially contrasting harmfulness interpretations under similar semantic conditions, highlighting that semantically related patterns may lead to different judgments depending on their context. By linking rules with opposing verdicts, these edges facilitate the retrieval of contrastive evidence for examining rule applicability and distinguishing harmful from harmless interpretations. Together, these cross-rule relations complement the rule-centered structural edges by enabling semantic retrieval ex-pansion and polarity-aware knowledge exploration. The re-sulting graph integrates local rule-level evidence with cross-rule semantic associations, providing a structured knowledge foundation for subsequent Cognitive RAG. 2) Training-Alignment Cognitive RAG: To further improve the quality of knowledge retrieval and reasoning, we develop MemeLogos Align, a training-aligned variant of Cognitive RAG through two optimization strategies: keywords alignment and reasoning alignment. 

Keywords alignment. To enhance the consistency of core semantic attribute extraction, we perform supervised fine-tuning using logical primitives derived from annotated rules as supervision targets. This process guides the model to map mul-timodal meme inputs into structured semantic representations aligned with the attribute schema of the harmfulness knowl-edge graph, rather than unconstrained textual descriptions. By reducing the semantic mismatch between extracted queries and graph attributes, keyword alignment aims to improve retrieval precision and the relevance of retrieved rules. 

Reasoning alignment. To further improve the utilization of retrieved knowledge during cognitive reasoning, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [38] to optimize the model through outcome-based feedback. For each meme, the model samples a group of candidate reasoning trajectories, each producing a final harmfulness verdict. We assign an accuracy-based reward according to the correctness of the predicted verdict: 

ri,g = I[ˆ yi,g = yi], (10) where ˆyi,g denotes the verdict produced by the g-th trajec-tory and yi is the ground-truth label. Through group-relative optimization, this reward encourages reasoning trajectories that lead to accurate harmfulness judgments, facilitating more effective integration of independent semantic interpretations and retrieved rule-level knowledge. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we propose MemeLogos, a knowledge-driven retrieval-augmented reasoning framework for harmful meme detection. Unlike conventional approaches relying on instance-level retrieval or unstructured explanations, MemeLogos ab-stracts recurring harmfulness patterns from CoT-annotated memes into reusable rule-level knowledge and organizes them into a structured harmfulness knowledge graph with explicit semantic and verdict-polarity-aware relations. Building upon this knowledge representation, Cognitive RAG integrates in-dependent meme interpretation, graph-grounded contrastive retrieval, and deliberative reasoning to enable more compre-hensive harmfulness analysis and judgment. Extensive ex-periments demonstrate that MemeLogos effectively improves harmful meme detection performance. Furthermore, ablation studies and interpretability evaluations highlight the contri-butions of structured knowledge organization and cognitive reasoning to more accurate predictions and evidence-grounded explanations. Overall, this work demonstrates the value of abstracting instance-level semantic knowledge into reusable, structured reasoning units, providing an effective approach to knowledge-enhanced multimodal harmful content detection.
