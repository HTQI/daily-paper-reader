# Abstract 

Relational out-of-distribution errors are easy to describe and hard to evaluate cleanly. An image may be natural, a caption may be fluent, and all mentioned objects may be common, yet the caption may describe the wrong relation in the image. Many evaluations intended to test this behavior can be solved without checking the pairing at all, because negatives differ in caption style, template, length, object frequency, or image statistics. We propose counterfactual pairing cycles, a blocked evaluation primitive that reuses the same two images and the same two captions on both sides of the comparison, changing only which image is paired with which caption. This makes image-only, text-only, and additive component shortcuts cancel exactly in finite samples. The same contrast also gives a training loss for relational outlier exposure and a diagnostic for bilinear vision-language scores. Across synthetic controls, GQA, and COCO, cycles separate coarse image-caption matching from directional role-binding failures, while also identifying geometric size relations that remain strongly visible in global features. In a shortcut-contaminated COCO outlier-exposure stress test, a model selected by a near-perfect shortcut validation AUROC retains substantial relational signal but remains below cycle training on exact cycles; cycle training improves CycleAcc from 0.882 to 

0.927 . The main lesson is simple: to test whether a model understands a pairing, the benchmark should keep the parts fixed, change the pairing, and ask only about coupling. The official implementation is available online. 

# 1 Introduction 

A wrong image-caption pair can look perfectly ordinary in isolation. The image is a real photograph. The caption is grammatical. The objects are familiar. The failure appears only when the two are put together: the caption assigns an attribute to the wrong object, reverses a spatial relation, or describes an event that belongs to a different image. This is the relational OOD problem we study. It is not an anomaly of the image alone or the caption alone. It is an anomaly of their coupling. This distinction has become important as vision-language models are deployed as general-purpose perceptual systems. Recent evaluations show that strong multimodal models can struggle with fine-grained composition, natural adversarial examples, spatial reasoning, and object-relation binding [4, 18, 13, 17, 3, 15, 27]. These failures are small in form but large in consequence: a model may recognize the objects and still miss who is doing what to whom, which object is on the left, or which caption belongs to the image. The difficulty is not only that relational errors are hard for models. They are also hard to evaluate cleanly. Many benchmarks that test multimodal reasoning contain easier signals. If negative captions are shorter, come from a different generator, use a special template, or mention rarer objects, a text-only detector can succeed. If negative images differ in style or category distribution, an image-only detector can succeed. Similar concerns have motivated efforts to build vision-indispensable,

adversarial, bidirectional, and composition-focused benchmarks for vision-language models [4, 18, 22, 13, 17, 25]. Related work on attribute recognition and spurious correlation likewise shows that label imbalance, component bias, and object co-occurrence can provide alternatives to learning the intended coupling [34–38]. The common lesson is that high benchmark performance can mislead when evaluation does not control what information the model is allowed to use. This issue is especially sharp for OOD detection. Classical and reconstruction-based OOD methods already show that the statistic used for training and validation determines what kind of abnormality is learned [10, 19, 21, 33]. Modern OOD methods increasingly use vision-language representations, negative labels, prompt tuning, and multimodal cues [16, 32, 30, 6, 25]. These methods are valuable, but the standard OOD framing often asks whether an example looks unlike the training distribution. Relational OOD asks a different question. The image and the caption may both look in-distribution. What is out-of-distribution is the pairing. A detector that succeeds by scoring the image and caption separately may therefore solve the wrong problem. We propose a small change in the unit of evaluation. Instead of comparing arbitrary positive and negative pairs, we compare two ways of pairing the same components. Take two valid image-caption pairs. Now keep the two images and the two captions fixed, but swap the captions across images. This creates a complete four-entry block: two original pairs and two swapped pairs. We retain the block only when the swapped pairs are verified invalid by the construction annotations or filters. The test then asks a single question: does the model prefer the original pairing over the swapped pairing? The positive side and the negative side contain exactly the same images and exactly the same captions. Only the assignment between images and captions changes. As a result, image-only cues, text-only cues, and any additive combination of such component cues cancel inside the block. The benchmark no longer asks whether either side looks unusual. It asks whether the two sides match. This blocked comparison is the central object of the paper. We call it a counterfactual pairing cycle .“Counterfactual” refers to the swapped pairings: the caption is fluent and the image is natural, but their association is wrong. “Cycle” refers to the complete four-entry structure: each image is paired once with its true caption and once with the other caption, and each caption is paired once with its true image and once with the other image. This symmetry is what makes the test insensitive to marginal shortcuts. We make three contributions: 1. We formalize counterfactual pairing cycles as a blocked evaluation primitive for relational OOD. In every block, the positive and negative sides have identical image and caption marginals. 2. We characterize the finite-sample balance condition required to remove all additive image-only and text-only scores: a signed comparison cancels such scores if and only if every image and every caption has zero signed degree. The 2 × 2 counterfactual pairing cycle is the minimal nondegenerate block satisfying this condition. 3. We use the same contrast as a training loss for relational outlier exposure and as a diagnostic for bilinear dual-encoder scores. The bilinear cycle score factorizes into paired image and text differences. Also, we evaluate the protocol on synthetic controls, GQA, and COCO. Cycles separate coarse matching from several strict role-binding failures and show why shortcut validation can overstate relational transfer.

# 3 Learning and diagnostics 

Cycle loss. The same contrast can be used for training via 

Lcyc (θ; C) = softplus( −∆cyc ,C (sθ )) .

Because ∆cyc ,C (s + a(u) + b(v)) = ∆ cyc ,C (s) for every cycle, arbitrary image-only and text-only heads cannot improve this objective. This differs from a four-entry BCE over the two valid and two swapped pairs, which depends on absolute scores and can reward component shifts that carry no relational information. 

Bilinear scores. Let ϕ : U → Rdϕ and ψ : V → Rdψ denote image and text embeddings, respectively. For a bilinear score 

s(u, v ) = ϕ(u)⊤M ψ (v), M ∈ Rdϕ×dψ ,

the cycle contrast factorizes as 

∆cyc ,C (s) = [ ϕ(u1) − ϕ(u2)] ⊤M [ψ(v1) − ψ(v2)] .

Thus rank sweeps distinguish two bottlenecks: if the relevant image and text differences are present but spread across directions, increasing the rank of M can help; if the representations do not contain the needed difference, increasing rank will not recover it. 

# 4 Constructing exact cycles 

An exact cycle must satisfy two requirements: both swaps are invalid for the intended relation, and the full four-entry block is available. We therefore use conservative dataset-specific construction rules and then apply a complete-block filter. Each retained block contains two valid pairs and their two swaps, with labels 1, 1, 0, 0; if any entry is missing, required annotations are unavailable, or a swap cannot be verified as invalid, the entire block is discarded. The construction principle is shared across datasets. For GQA [14], we instantiate caption-like facts from scene graphs and retain a block only when both swapped image-fact pairings are invalid under the annotation-derived predicate. For COCO object cycles, a swapped caption is invalid if it mentions a selected MSCOCO category [20], under a fixed synonym table, that is has no corresponding annotation in the target image. For COCO relation cycles, captions are generated from instance boxes; the source relation must hold under a fixed geometric margin, and in strict splits the target image must contain both mentioned categories while making the swapped relation false under the same rule. Construction annotations are used only for filtering and are not given to the detector. Because every retained block reuses the same two images and captions on both sides, the positive and negative image and caption marginals match exactly within each block and after aggregation. Caption-length marginals also match whenever caption strings are reused. Appendix D reports the corresponding total-variation audits. 

# 5 Experiments 

Our experiments are diagnostic. We first check that the cycle construction preserves the signed-degree balance that removes additive component-level leakage in settings where the answer is known. We 5then use exact cycles to examine what frozen CLIP-like representations expose: coarse image-caption compatibility, geometric relations, or strict role binding. Finally, we test whether an outlier-exposure model selected by a shortcut validation signal transfers to relational cycles. Unless otherwise stated, raw-image experiments use frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 image and text fea-tures [24]. We evaluate image-only, text-only, and additive logistic probes as implementation audits rather than competitive baselines: by Theorem 1, exact cycles force any additive score to tie, so chance performance verifies that the retained blocks, filtering, and feature availability have preserved the required signed-degree balance. We also evaluate zero-shot CLIP cosine; diagonal and bilinear interaction probes; and low-rank bilinear scores

5.1 Sanity checks: the audit behaves as intended 

Synthetic controls isolate the algebra from dataset details. Table 1 shows four checks. The pure-interaction row is used only to verify that marginal scores have chance performance when the shift is purely in the coupling; the interaction score is therefore not reported for that row. Additive probes are chance on pure interactions, but become perfect when a marginal shortcut is injected. Rank-constrained interaction probes improve from 0.6725 at rank 1 to 0.7658 at rank 8, then slightly decrease to 0.7575 at rank 16 . Bag representations discard binding information that pair-aware scores recover. These controls are unblocked two-sample diagnostics, so we report AUROC. The same signed-degree balance check holds on real exact-cycle splits: image-only, text-only, and additive probes are at chance under CycleAcc , as required for retained complete blocks. When we deliberately contaminate text marginals in an unblocked diagnostic, text-only and additive probes become perfect. Thus the cycle metric removes additive component plausibility as a route to success and rewards pair-dependent signal instead.

# 6 Related work 

Classical OOD detection uses confidence, density, reconstruction, or energy-based scores [10, 19, 21, 33], while outlier exposure learns from auxiliary negatives [11]. These settings typically ask whether an example looks unlike the training distribution. Relational OOD instead asks whether two individually plausible components are paired correctly, making marginal shortcuts especially harmful: validation success from caption style, length, object frequency, or image-distribution artifacts may overestimate transfer to wrong-pairing errors. Similar shortcut issues arise in hypothesis-only and question-only baselines for NLI and VQA [9, 23, 1]. Work on pedestrian attribute recognition has likewise shown that label imbalance, co-occurrence bias, and feature resampling can determine whether a model learns the intended attribute rather than a correlated proxy [35, 34, 36, 37]; related analysis asks when spurious-correlation removal is learnable at all [38]. Compositional vision-language benchmarks such as Winoground, ARO, and SugarCrepe use paired comparisons to test word order, binding, and relational understanding [28, 31, 12]. BiVLC extends this evaluation to bidirectional retrieval [22], while CLoVe directly improves compositional language encoding in contrastive models [2]. Our distinction is the invariant statistic used to score the comparison. Theorem 1 shows that exact cancellation of image-only and text-only scores requires zero signed degree for every image and caption in the reported comparison. Counterfactual pairing cycles enforce this condition locally with a complete 2 × 2 block and score it by the signed interaction contrast. Thus the primitive is not a pooled matched/mismatched ranking set, but a finite-sample marginally balanced test for relational OOD. The training and diagnostic uses connect to contrastive learning [29, 5] and interaction or dependence testing [7, 8, 26], but target pair-level relational scoring under exact marginal cancellation. 97 Conclusion 

Relational out-of-distribution evaluation requires evidence about the pairing, not only evidence that each component is plausible. Counterfactual pairing cycles provide a finite-sample block design in which image-only, text-only, and arbitrary additive component scores cancel exactly. The same interaction contrast yields a training objective and a family of diagnostics for bilinear vision-language scores. Across synthetic controls, COCO, and GQA, the diagnostics separate coarse object compatibility from stricter spatial and role-binding structure. Coarse COCO cycles remain easy, strict left/right is weak for the evaluated bilinear probes, and apparent size is substantially more visible. In the marker-controlled COCO study, Cycle OE obtains 0.9270 ± 0.0075 exact CycleAcc, while shortcut-contaminated BCE obtains 0.8817 ± 0.0021 . Because these protocols differ in objective, validation criterion, and marker exposure, the comparison should not be read as isolating a single causal factor. Exact CycleAcc measures sensitivity to pair-dependent structure within a complete block. It does not certify full semantic understanding, and its conclusions depend on the cycle predicates and representations being evaluated. The public implementation enables the same audits to be applied to additional encoders, benchmarks, and relation families.

# D Dataset construction and exact audits 

D.1 Cycle-construction principle 

Each validation example is a four-pair cycle 

(u1, v 1), (u2, v 2), (u1, v 2), (u2, v 1).

The first two pairs are valid matched pairs under the relevant dataset predicate. The last two pairs are obtained by exchanging the two candidates and are invalid under the same predicate. Candidate strata are constructed so that the exchange does not create an explicit marginal identity difference between matched and cross-paired candidates. For every family, the builder applies three checks: 1. Matched validity. Both matched pairs satisfy the family predicate. 2. Cross-pair invalidity. Both exchanged pairs violate the family predicate. 3. Marginal balance. The exchanged candidates are drawn from the same candidate stratum as the matched candidates. The released implementation contains the exact predicates and filtering configuration for every family. 

D.2 COCO cycle families 

The COCO diagnostics use frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 image and caption features. The cycle families progress from coarse object compatibility to increasingly specific relational compatibility: • Coarse object cycles. Cross-pairs violate coarse image–caption object compatibility. • Matched object cycles. Candidate captions are matched on coarse object evidence, while the exchanged pairs violate the more specific cycle predicate. • Box-relation cycles. Object bounding boxes determine the relevant spatial configuration, and the exchange disrupts that configuration. • Strict spatial cycles. The predicate requires a strict left/right configuration rather than a coarse co-occurrence pattern. • Strict size cycles. The predicate requires the relevant strict size relation between objects. This progression separates object presence from relational structure. The coarse, matched, box, and strict families therefore test different levels of pair dependence while retaining the same four-pair cycle form. 

D.3 GQA cycle families 

The GQA diagnostics are constructed from scene-graph annotations. The reported families cover attribute, relation/object, and binding structure. Attribute cycles test whether an object-level attribute is coupled to the correct scene content. Relation/object cycles test whether a relation and its object arguments remain correctly coupled. Binding cycles are intended to test whether an attribute remains bound to the correct object when multiple objects are present. The binding family does not yield enough validation cycles under the prespecified minimum-cycle requirement: only 18 –19 validation cycles are available. We therefore report this family as unavailable rather than treating an extremely small cycle set as a stable diagnostic. 17 D.4 Exact audit summary

# F Broader impact 

This work provides a diagnostic framework for determining whether a multimodal compatibility score uses pair-dependent structure rather than only marginal image or caption evidence. The primary positive impact is measurement reliability: exact cycle tests can prevent unimodal shortcuts from being misinterpreted as compositional or relational understanding. This is relevant to retrieval, visual question answering, medical and scientific image–text analysis, and other settings where incorrect pairings can have consequential interpretations. The same diagnostics could also be used to identify benchmark weaknesses or to design harder shortcuts. This dual-use risk is typical of evaluation research. We mitigate it by emphasizing exact audits, public reproducibility, and explicit reporting of cases where a probe remains near chance or unavailable. The work does not introduce a surveillance system, generate personal data, or target identifiable individuals. 

# G Limitations 

The study has several limitations. First, the empirical analysis uses frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 representations and two widely used bench-marks, COCO and GQA. The results characterize the interaction information available in this setting; they do not imply that every multimodal encoder exhibits the same behavior. Second, CycleAcc tests a specific atomic 2×2 interaction. Exact failure of additive and unimodal con-trols is a strength of the metric, but a single cycle family cannot capture every form of compositionality. Different cycle predicates may probe different visual or linguistic evidence. Third, cycle construction depends on the quality of the underlying annotations. The exact audits rule out measured marginal imbalance and predicate-validity violations, but they do not remove all semantic ambiguity inherent in natural captions or scene graphs. Fourth, the GQA binding family is unavailable because too few validation cycles survive the con-struction and minimum-size requirements. The paper therefore does not make a binding-specific empirical claim for GQA. Finally, the Shortcut OE result shows that marker-contaminated OE training can retain substantial cycle structure. The experiments support a modest, consistent transfer gap in this setting, not a universal claim that OE either preserves or destroys relational transfer under every model, objective, and contamination mechanism. 20 H Reproducibility statement
