Abstract 

Memes play a central role in modern online communication, especially in contexts of polit-ical conflict where visual and textual cues com-bine to convey persuasive messages. We study persuasive technique detection in Ukrainian memes using vision–language models (VLMs). We introduce UkrMeme, a dataset of 200 or-ganically produced Ukrainian wartime memes annotated for persuasion techniques, and PTM-UA, a Ukrainian translation of a subset of the Propaganda 950 benchmark that preserves orig-inal visual content while translating meme text. We evaluate VLMs in a zero-shot prompting setup across image, text, and multimodal inputs using hierarchical F1 metrics. Finally, we com-pare Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) and model-generated token attributions and an-alyze the semantic categories of evidence em-phasized by each explanation method. 

1 Introduction 

Memes have become one of the central commu-nicative form in modern information ecosystem, combining visual and textual signals into compact multimodal artifacts. In contexts of political con-flict and war, memes often serve as weapon of soft propaganda, especially easy to plant for for-eign audiences, but it can also be instrument of collective sense-making, identity construction, and psychological coping (Liagusha and Iarovyi, 2025; of Excellence, 2015; Mejova et al., 2025). Since the beginning of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Ukrainian online spaces have witnessed an unprecedented proliferation of war-related memes. Scholars and journalists have noted that Ukrainian meme culture functions as a form of grassroots resilience, black humor, and communal morale-building rather than merely as an instrument of coordinated information warfare (Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism, 2023; Bilaniuk, 2023; Bondarenko, 2025; Bouchard and Antsybor, 2025). While detection of persuasion techniques in mul-timodal content has recently received increased attention in Natural Language Processing (NLP), most existing benchmarks are English-centric and focus on identifying persuasive techniques in po-litical messaging (Dimitrov et al., 2021). Cross-lingual and culturally grounded analyses remain limited, especially for low-resource languages and contexts of active conflict. Moreover, the major-ity of prior work emphasizes model performance, with comparatively less attention to faithfulness and explainability of model decisions. In this work, we investigate how large lan-guage models (LLMs) and vision-language mod-els (VLMs) identify such techniques in memes in Ukrainian. Importantly, while we use persua-sion labels from propaganda studies, this study does not assume that Ukrainian memes constitute state-coordinated propaganda, but a form of grass-roots resilience. Hence, we treat persuasion tech-niques as an analytical lens for examining how persuasive rhetoric and emotional framing emerge organically in grassroots digital creativity during wartime. Our focus is on understanding both model behavior and the rhetorical structure of Ukrainian meme discourse. We construct two complementary datasets: (i) we translate into Ukrainian a subset of a widely used multimodal persuasive meme bench-mark (Dimitrov et al., 2021), preserving images while translating meme text into Ukrainian. This controlled setup allows us to isolate language ef-fects while holding visual content constant; (ii) we compile and annotate a novel dataset of 200 organi-cally produced Ukrainian wartime memes, curated from public online sources. Each meme is indepen-dently annotated by two native Ukrainian speakers using a multi-label taxonomy, followed by adju-dication to ensure consistency. 2 Related Work 

Automatic detection of persuasion techniques has been widely studied in textual political discourse. Early work focused on news articles and politi-cal speeches, introducing fine-grained taxonomies of persuasive techniques and corresponding super-vised benchmarks (Da San Martino et al., 2019a; Dmitriev et al., 2019). The labels and annotation quality for this task has been disputed as subjective and usually low-annotator agreement is expected (Sahitaj et al., 2025). With the rise of multimodal communication, attention has shifted toward propa-ganda in internet memes, where meaning emerges from the interaction between text and image (Dim-itrov et al., 2021; Kmainasi et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025). These works demonstrate much lower per-formance than purely textual propaganda detection. At the same time, multimodal models were shown to outperform text-only systems on meme classifi-cation tasks, particularly when OCR-extracted text is incorporated into the visual pipeline. Notably,

However, existing benchmarks remain pre-dominantly English-centric, while other languages and cross-lingual transfer in memes remain under-explored, especially in culturally specific contexts such as wartime meme production. To our knowledge, no prior work systemati-cally evaluates persuasion technique detection in Ukrainian memes under a prompt-only setting or compares text-only and vision-language models using faithfulness-oriented explainability metrics. 

3 Methods 

3.1 Data 

We utilize two distinct datasets for our experiments: a translated subset of the Propaganda 950 dataset from Dimitrov et al. (2021), PTM-UA (Propa-ganda Techniques in Meme-Ukrainian) and a newly collected dataset of Ukrainian memes, U KR MEME .

3.1.1 Translated Propaganda 950 (PTM-UA) 

To obtain a Ukrainian-language evaluation set, we translate the test split of the Propaganda 950 dataset (Dimitrov et al., 2021) using a semi-automated pipeline. The process consists of four stages: (1) text detection using EasyOCR 2, (2) re-moval of detected text via LaMa inpainting (Su-vorov et al., 2022) 3, (3) translation of the extracted English text into Ukrainian with GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025), prompted to preserve rhetorical intent while producing concise, colloquial output, and (4) ren-dering the translated text back onto the image using automatic font sizing, text wrapping, and adaptive color selection for readability. After the automated pipeline, we manually re-view all 200 translated test samples. We discard samples where the text placement was unsatisfac-tory (e.g., text overflowing bounding boxes or over-lapping with important visual elements), where the inpainting process removed crucial visual informa-tion, or where the translation was inaccurate. The majority of rejected samples suffered from poor text placement, with a smaller number lost to in-painting artifacts and very few due to translation errors. After filtering, we retain 158 samples for evaluation. The label distribution for the translated

3.2 Experimental set-up 

In this section, we describe our approach to persua-sion detection and the explainability analysis we conduct using two different methods: Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) (Bach et al., 2015) and VLM-based token attribution. 

3.2.1 Motivation 

LRP and VLM-based token attribution represent fundamentally different explainability approaches. LRP propagates relevance scores backward through the network, assigning importance based on each token’s mathematical contribution to the output, a model-internal, gradient-based explanation. How-ever, LRP explanations depend strongly on the 3Figure 3: Label distribution for the U KR MEME dataset (200 samples). 

> Figure 4: Label distribution for the translated PTM-UA (158 samples).

chosen propagation rules and model architecture, meaning that analysis may reflect methodological choices rather than the model’s true decision pro-cess. Token attribution for Language models relies on the model’s own reasoning to identify important tokens as part of its generation, which is a linguis-tic, model-external explanation. We hypothesize that comparing these complementary explanation methods can reveal systematic differences in the evidence emphasized by each approach. 

3.2.2 Models 

We evaluate four models across two tiers. Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct 

(Qwen Team, 2025) are used for both persuasion de-tection and explainability analysis: as open-weight models, they provide full access to internal activa-tions, enabling LRP alongside VLM-based token attribution on the same model. Comparing the two sizes additionally reveals scale effects on detection and explanation quality. We also report detection-only results for two additional models: GPT-4.1-mini and Gemma 3 4B . These are excluded from the explainability analysis for different reasons de-tailed in Appendix B. 

3.2.3 Detection Metrics 

We report hierarchical F1 following (Kiritchenko et al., 2006; Dimitrov et al., 2024), which awards partial credit when predicted and gold labels share ancestors in the taxonomy. The taxonomy or-ganizes 22 techniques under categories such as Ethos, Pathos, Logos, and Ad Hominem (Dim-itrov et al., 2024). Each label set is extended to include all ancestor nodes except the root (Persua-sion), and precision, recall, and F1 are computed over these extended sets. We report three vari-ants: micro-averaged (the official SemEval metric), macro-averaged over taxonomy nodes, and mean per-sample F1. Full definitions are provided in Appendix E. 

3.2.4 Baselines and translation control 

We evaluate a weighted-random baseline to contex-tualize model performance. For each persuasion label, a binary prediction is sampled independently according to its empirical positive frequency in the dataset, and results are averaged over 30 runs. We report the same hierarchical micro- and macro-F1 metrics as for the evaluated models. Additionally, to assess whether performance on PTM-UA is affected by translation into Ukrainian, we evaluate all four models on the original En-glish versions of the same 158 Propaganda 950 samples retained in PTM-UA after filtering. We use identical input modalities and prompting set-tings, enabling a paired comparison in which meme identity and visual content are held constant while the textual condition differs between the original English and translated Ukrainian versions. 

3.2.5 Explanation Setup 

Since our datasets consist of visual content (images containing text), we evaluate across three input modes: (i) image , where only the image is pro-vided and the model must read text from it; (ii) 

image+text , where both the image and its extracted text are provided in the prompt; and (iii) text , where only the extracted text is provided without the im-age. This allows us to assess how input modality affects both detection performance and the result-ing explanations. 

Prompting. We use two zero-shot prompts: one for the image mode and another shared by the im-age+text and text modes. Both prompts provide the full set of persuasion technique classes and instruct the model to return a JSON object with three fields: labels (predicted persuasion tech-niques), referred_words (individual words from the meme text most relevant to the classification), and referred_image_concepts (up to five visual 4elements supporting the classification, ranked by importance). Full prompts are provided in Ap-pendix C. While the prompt also elicits image-level concept descriptions, we restrict our explainability analysis to textual token attributions due to the diffi-culty of systematically comparing free-text concept outputs with LRP spatial heatmaps. 

LRP extraction. We use lxt.efficient with 

zennit to monkey-patch the linear, gated-MLP, RMSNorm, LayerNorm, and attention layers of the Qwen3-VL text decoder with AttnLRP propaga-tion rules (Achtibat et al., 2024) (library defaults, unmodified), replacing plain gradients in the back-ward pass. For each sample, we re-run a forward pass from the cached generated token sequence, with gradients enabled on the input embeddings, and set the propagation target as the sum of logits at the predicted label token(s). Relevance is the embedding times its gradient, summed over the em-bedding dimension, then normalized per sample by its max absolute value (non-finite values clipped to zero). For image+text , this backward pass uses text tokens only; visual features are not re-injected. To obtain word-level scores, we align the OCR text with the decoded prompt via tokenizer offsets, as-sign each overlapping subword token to the word containing its span midpoint, and average absolute relevance per word. Words are filtered (stopwords, punctuation, length 1), and the number of LRP words kept per sample is capped to match the count of words in the model’s own verbalized explanation for that sample (max k = 5 ), for comparison. 

Word-level comparison. We compare VLM-attributed and LRP-selected words using Jaccard similarity to assess the agreement between the two explanation methods. 

Category analysis. To characterize the types of explanations each method produces, we categorize tokens into five categories: E NTITY (named peo-ple, places, organizations, brands), E MOTIONAL 

(emotionally loaded or fear/hate/outrage-inducing language), R HETORIC (calls to action, imperatives, slogans, persuasive framing), C ONTENT (neutral factual nouns, events, dates, statistics), and O THER 

(numbers, punctuation artifacts, unclear items) us-ing GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024). We then compare the category distributions across methods. The catego-rization prompt is provided in Appendix D, and its accuracy was validated on a manually annotated sample.

4.3 Effect of Input Modality 

On PTM-UA, image+text yields the best F1 for the 4B model across all averaging methods, confirming that supplying extracted text reduces OCR errors. The 8B model’s image-only performance is com-petitive with image+text (micro 0.524 vs. 0.523), suggesting its stronger OCR partially closes the gap. Text-only consistently lags behind, indicating visual context carries non-trivial signal. On UkrMeme, modality effects diverge by met-ric. Image and image+text modes achieve higher micro F1 through improved recall, but text-only yields the best per-sample F1 for both 4B (0.277) and 8B (0.388). The image modes over-predict labels, with higher recall coming at the cost of substantially lower precision, penalizing individ-ual samples. The model interprets visual cues in wartime memes as additional evidence for per-suasion techniques, leading to systematic over-labeling. 

4.4 Effect of Model Scale 

Scaling from 4B to 8B yields uneven gains. On PTM-UA, the 8B model improves image-only mi-cro F1 (0.524 vs. 0.483) and achieves the best macro F1 (0.291), indicating better rare-technique coverage. On UkrMeme, the largest gain appears in text-only per-sample F1 (0.388 vs. 0.277, a 40% relative improvement). In multimodal modes, gains are modest on both datasets. 

4.5 No Propaganda Label Ablation 

The original Propaganda 950 taxonomy does not include a No Propaganda label; we add it to the prompt as an explicit option. In PTM-UA only 2 samples genuinely lack annotations, yet both models predict No Propaganda for 31 samples in text-only mode. Removing this option (Table 7 in Appendix A) yields the largest gains in text-only mode; image and image+text improvements are marginal. The 4B model is more responsive: of the 31 text-only No Propaganda predictions, only 8 become empty when the label is removed, with the rest replaced by technique labels. The 8B model is more conservative (22 of 31 become empty rather than committing to a label).

4.7 LRP and token attribution Dataset related differences. Across the four Qwen settings, the token-level explanations show a clear split between the translated and native Ukrainian meme conditions. In the translated dataset, both VLM-based token attribution and LRP are dominated by tokens tied to the orig-inal English political discourse (e.g. "trump", "democrats"). This pattern is stable for both text+image and text-only settings, suggesting that translation preserves the main lexical persuasion cues but also keeps the dataset anchored in the source-domain. In contrast, the native Ukrainian dataset is much more centered on the local wartime and sociopolitical context, with frequent tokens such as "ukrainians", "missiles", "drones", "Rus-sia", "Putin", "alert", and "Tomahawk".

Explanation agreement varies across dataset set-tings. Native multimodal memes exhibit the high-est median overlap between VLM explanations and LRP attributions, suggesting more stable evidence selection. In contrast, translated memes show lower and more variable agreement, indicating that trans-lation artifacts and multimodal context may desta-bilize token-level attribution alignment. 

Modality differences. The comparison between text+image and text-only also reveals an important modality effect. In the translated condition, the two modes are fairly similar, which suggests that much of the signal is recoverable from OCR text alone. In the native Ukrainian condition, however, the text+image model produces a broader and less purely lexical set of salient tokens, including more context-dependent tokens like "watch", "people", "works", "self", and "day", whereas the text-only model concentrates more on explicit textual trig-gers such as "missiles", "Trump", "Russia", and "Putin." This indicates that for native memes, the visual channel contributes additional contextual ev-idence that is not reducible to extracted text. 

Low accuracy settings. The low-hF1 (< 0.2) ex-amples are especially informative. A recurring pattern is that VLM-based token attribution and LRP often overlap strongly even when the predic-tion is wrong, with several cases showing very high Jaccard agreement, even up to 1.0. 4.8 Category Analysis of Attributed tokens 

We categorize all attributed tokens into five seman-tic categories (E NTITY , E MOTIONAL , R HETORIC ,CONTENT , O THER ; see Section 3) using GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) with a structured categorization prompt (Appendix D), validated at 80.3% accuracy on a manually annotated sample of 61 tokens. We partition tokens into shared (selected by both meth-ods), VLM-only , and LRP-only groups (Table 5). The two methods select qualitatively different evidence. VLM-attributed tokens are dispropor-tionately E MOTIONAL (33.7% on PTM-UA, 32.4% on UkrMeme) and R HETORIC (22.7%, 17.6%), suggesting that the model’s verbalized reasoning gravitates toward affectively loaded and persuasive-sounding language when justifying its prediction. The O THER cate-gory is also elevated (14.2% on PTM-UA, 21.3% on UkrMeme), reflecting LRP’s tendency to assign relevance to function words and punctuation that carry mathematical but not semantic importance. Shared tokens have the highest E NTITY propor-tion ( ∼21-23%) and lowest O THER (∼4-5%), in-dicating that when both methods converge, they agree on named entities, specific people, organi-zations, and places, as anchors for classification. These patterns are consistent across datasets despite strong differences in content and language, with the main divergence being a higher O THER ratio in UkrMeme’s LRP-only group, likely reflecting noisier OCR output in native Ukrainian memes. 

5 Discussion and Conclusion 

Our results highlight both the potential and lim-itations of current vision–language models for analyzing multimodal political discourse in low-resource and culturally specific contexts. While models achieve moderate performance on trans-lated memes derived from English benchmarks, per-formance drops substantially on native Ukrainian memes, suggesting that cultural knowledge and contextual grounding remain key bottlenecks. In-put modality also plays a complex role: multimodal inputs improve recall but often lead to systematic over-prediction of persuasion techniques, particu-larly in emotionally charged wartime content. In-creasing model scale yields uneven gains, with im-provements primarily in text-only settings rather than multimodal reasoning. Finally, our explain-ability analysis reveals a consistent divergence be-tween model-based token attribution reasoning and LRP-based attributions: verbal explanations em-phasize emotional and rhetorical cues, while inter-nal relevance signals focus more on content words. Together, these findings underscore the challenges of reliable persuasion detection and interpretation in real-world multimodal settings. 9Limitations 

Our study has several limitations. First, both datasets are relatively small: PTM-UA contains 158 translated memes and UkrMeme 200 native Ukrainian memes. While sufficient for exploratory evaluation, this scale limits statistical power and will not capture the full diversity of Ukrainian meme culture. Second, persuasion technique anno-tation is inherently subjective, and although inter-annotator agreement is moderate, disagreement re-flects genuine ambiguity in rhetorical interpretation rather than purely annotation error, which is, how-ever, conforming with reported agreement problem in prior work. Third, the translated PTM-UA dataset preserves visual content from English memes while replac-ing text with Ukrainian translations. While this design isolates language effects, it may introduce translation artifacts and does not fully reproduce authentic Ukrainian meme discourse. Although originally motivated as a possible route toward data augmentation, our results suggest that sim-ple translation-based augmentation is unlikely to provide substantial benefits. Moreover, the labels, coming from propaganda research, may not capture all of the discourse methods genuine civic society creation contains. Future work should analyse the memes in partnership with social scientists and lin-guists to identify genre-specific phenomena. Fourth, our experiments rely on zero-shot prompting and therefore do not evaluate supervised or fine-tuned systems. Fifth, explainability analysis is restricted to the two Qwen3-VL models: GPT-4.1-mini is excluded because LRP requires access to model-internal activations unavailable through a closed API, and Gemma 3 4B was excluded be-cause running LRP over a 4B-parameter model for the full dataset was not computationally fea-sible within the scope of this study. Finally, our explainability analysis focuses on textual token at-tribution because visual concept explanations are difficult to compare systematically across meth-ods. Future work should extend faithfulness anal-ysis to spatial visual explanations, larger datasets, and open-weight models of comparable size to the closed-API baselines. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies persuasion techniques in memes created during an ongoing armed conflict. Impor-tantly, we do not assume that Ukrainian memes constitute coordinated propaganda; rather, we treat persuasion techniques as an analytical framework for examining rhetorical strategies in grassroots digital communication. Ukrainian wartime memes often serve functions of humor, resilience, and col-lective coping rather than strategic information war-fare. While empirically our annotation procedure showed that persuasion techniques as a set of labels provided a sufficiently expressive annotation frame-work encoded in UkrMeme dataset, with consider-ation of the sensitivity of the current war context, we recognize that some readers can find utilization of propaganda and persuasion techniques insulting. All memes in the UkrMeme dataset were col-lected from publicly accessible online sources. The dataset is used exclusively for research purposes and does not include private or personally identifi-able information. Nevertheless, memes may con-tain politically sensitive or emotionally charged content. We therefore frame our analysis as de-scriptive rather than normative and caution against using automated persuasion detection systems as tools for censorship or political moderation. We acknowledge the broader risk that such research can be misused to suppress legitimate political ex-pression. Our goal is to improve transparency and understanding of the phenomena, not to enable au-tomated policing of political speech.
